Students in higher education plan their studies against curricula that encode
prerequisites, electives and institutional rules. The tools that support this
planning are split in two. Graph-based systems draw the structure of a
curriculum but leave the timeline aside; table-based planners arrange courses
across semesters and check the result against the regulations, but leave the
structure implicit. Where both have been brought into one tool, dependency
information has been embedded in the schedule itself, which ties it to a
chronological layout.

This thesis designs, builds and evaluates a hybrid graph--table interface in
which a browsable curriculum graph and a schedule-managing table write to one
shared plan. A formative study with six students,
conducted around an interactive but non-functional prototype, yields a ranked
specification of 13 features carrying 55 numbered requirements. The tool is then
evaluated with eleven students, each planning once without the Graph View and
once with it, observed through a frame-sampled record of the interface, a
think-aloud transcript and post-task questionnaires.

The exploratory evaluation with eleven students revealed a consistent,
checklist-driven planning loop across all experience levels. The graph replaced
the course catalogue for finding candidates, while courses continued to be
committed in the table. This split workflow made a Parking Stage essential as a
buffer to hold uncommitted courses between the two views. Furthermore, because
the curriculum encoded few strict dependencies, the graph's primary value
shifted from showing sequences to supporting orientation and filtered browsing.
Overall reported satisfaction was high, with the lowest metric (Perspicuity)
aligning precisely with difficulties observed during the tasks.
