\label{chap:introduction}
Students in higher education face growing challenges in planning their studies. Curricula often involve complex prerequisites, electives, and institutional rules that are difficult to navigate using static study guides or standard plans~\cite{schulte_large_2017, morsy_study_2019}. Individual circumstances, such as failed courses, varying study speeds, or limited access to advisors, further complicate planning and can affect performance, graduation times, and motivation~\cite{wong_sequence_2018}.

Existing approaches address these issues but remain limited. Graph-based systems focus mainly on visualising curriculum structure and \gterm{dependency}{course dependencies}~\cite{auvinen_stops_2014, rollande_graph_2013, trippel_developing_2025}, while table-based planning tools emphasise semester-level scheduling~\cite{hirmer_requirements_2022, judel_supporting_2023}, typically in isolation from one another. This motivates investigating a hybrid approach: integrating a graph and a table into a single planning workflow so students can understand structure and dependencies (graph) and manage their schedule and workload (table).

Two further strands report data back to the student rather than to the institution. Educational Dashboards (ED) visualise what a student has done and how far they have come~\cite{schwendimann2017, verbert_learning_2020, loboda_mastery_2014}, while Educational Recommender Systems (ERS) suggest what to take next~\cite{bhumichitr_recommender_2017, wong_sequence_2018, esteban_helping_2020, srisamutr_course_2018, al-badarenah_automated_2016}. \section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

Building on the challenges and gaps outlined above, this thesis investigates the design, implementation, and experience of a hybrid graph-table study planner, built for and evaluated against two degree programmes at the Faculty of Informatics of the Vienna University of Technology (TU~Wien): the Bachelor programme in Computer Science\footnote{\url{https://informatics.tuwien.ac.at/bachelor/curriculum-ue033521.pdf}} and the Master programme in Software Engineering \& Internet Computing\footnote{\url{https://informatics.tuwien.ac.at/master/curriculum-ue066937.pdf}}. We formulate three research questions:

\textbf{Research Question 1} \emph{What are students' needs and resulting requirements for a hybrid graph-table interface that supports programme-level study planning and understanding of curriculum structure and course dependencies?}

This question is addressed by a formative needfinding study conducted with an interactive but non-functional prototype (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}), whose findings are translated into a ranked features-and-requirements specification (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}). Throughout this thesis, \emph{course dependencies} means the structure a curriculum itself fixes, its containment hierarchy together with the hard constraints published alongside it; the glossary distinguishes this from the soft, recommended orderings a tool may curate. How much of either the two programmes in scope actually carry is itself a finding, reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

Building on these requirements, the second sub-question concerns the design and construction of the tool itself:

\textbf{Research Question 2} \emph{How can we design and implement a hybrid interface that combines graph-based visualisation with table-based planning to help students plan their studies and understand curriculum structure and course dependencies, based on those requirements?}

This question is addressed by the Design chapter (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}), which derives each design decision from a requirement, prior evidence, or an explicitly stated assumption, and by the Implementation chapter (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}), which realises that design as a working system.

Finally, the third sub-question examines how the implemented system is received by its intended users:

\textbf{Research Question 3} \emph{How do students experience the hybrid interface in terms of user experience (effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction)?}

This question is addressed by an evaluation study with students (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}) to assess the implemented tool and the Discussion (Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}).

    \item \textbf{Evidence that a completion signal students trust can certify a plan it has not checked.} Every participant reported success in a condition in which no plan met the stated constraints, and the tool agreed with them, because the constraints it does not model are silent rather than wrong. This bears on any planning tool that guides through a checklist (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:outlook}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} sets out the research paradigm, process, and scope that frame the work. Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} reviews related systems and establishes the research gap. Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study} reports the formative needfinding study, and Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements} translates its findings into a features-and-requirements specification; together these answer Research Question~1. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} and Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} design and build the hybrid tool, answering Research Question~2. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} evaluates the tool with students and Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} interprets what it found, together answering Research Question~3. Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} also states the answer to each of the three research questions in turn (Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq-answers}), and sets out the limitations that bound them and the directions for future work.
