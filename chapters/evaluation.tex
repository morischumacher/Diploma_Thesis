\label{chap:evaluation}
This chapter answers Research Question~3:

\begin{quote}
\textit{How do students plan with the hybrid interface, and what does the graph-based view contribute to their planning?}
\end{quote}

Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} names the three lenses the question is read through: the planning process as the recordings show it, the plans students produce, and the satisfaction they report. This chapter takes the process first, then the satisfaction reported, then the plans produced, so that what students made can be read against what they said.

The study was designed to observe one thing: what a curriculum graph contributes to the planning process of a student who already has a way of planning without it. The formative study had treated the value of a graph-based view as a premise, the first of the two assumptions stated in Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions}, and the design had assigned the two views different roles, exploration to the graph and commitment to the table (Section~\ref{sec:mutual-exclusion}). Neither had been tested. The study therefore has eleven students plan a comparable task twice with the tool of Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, first without the graph and then with it, and records not only what they report but what they do, so that the graph's contribution can be located in the process rather than inferred from a rating. That intention fixed the design, and so did the scale a master's thesis affords. A reliable estimate of the graph's effect on outcomes, on speed or on plan quality would need a counterbalanced sample several times larger than eleven, matched for curriculum knowledge and run without a facilitator; none of that was available. The choice was therefore made deliberately to study few students deeply rather than many shallowly: eleven sessions, two records per session, every frame of every recording labelled, and findings reported as descriptions and as hypotheses rather than as effects. The order of the two conditions is deliberate for the same reason, its cost was known in advance and is stated in Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}, and no claim in this chapter is one the order would compromise.

Section~\ref{sec:eval-design} states the design. Section~\ref{sec:eval-process} reconstructs the planning process the recordings show, and Section~\ref{sec:eval-ab} locates what the graph changes in it, which is the comparison the two conditions were run for. Section~\ref{sec:eval-around} covers the surfaces around that process, Section~\ref{sec:eval-findings} what worked and where the process breaks, Section~\ref{sec:eval-questionnaire} the questionnaires, Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness} the plans produced against self-report, and Section~\ref{sec:eval-summary} the conclusions Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} takes up. In the terms of the nested model, the study validates the encoding, interaction and algorithm levels of the artefact~\cite{munzner2009}.

\section{Study Design}
\label{sec:eval-design}

The study is a within-subjects, two-condition observation of eleven students planning a Bachelor curriculum under persona scenarios. This section states the conditions and the reason for their order, the system under test, the procedure and instruments, the cohort, and the analysis method on which every later figure rests.

\subsection{Conditions, Scenarios and Order}
\label{sec:eval-conditions}

Each participant completed two planning tasks, framed as persona scenarios so that individual domain preference does not vary the task, under two settings of the graph. The setting is an account-level property fixed before the task begins; participants did not switch views between conditions within a run.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scenario~A (graph off).} A Cybersecurity persona, planned with the graph unavailable. The table, Dashboard, recommendations, catalogue, and Parking Stage remain fully available.
    \item \textbf{Scenario~B (graph on).} An Artificial Intelligence persona, planned with the full tool including the graph and its filter sidebar.
\end{itemize}

Everything other than the graph was held constant across the two conditions. Each brief stated only its persona constraints, namely the per-semester ECTS band and the single reduced-load semester, together with strategy guidance on the kind of module to prefer. The curriculum's own hard requirements, that is the mandatory modules, the chosen focus area, the narrow (\emph{enge Wahl}) and broad (\emph{breite Wahl}) elective categories, and the introductory-phase (StEOP) rules, were never stated in the brief. Participants encountered them only inside the tool and tracked them through the Dashboard, which makes the Dashboard the sole channel through which the curriculum was communicated during a task. The two briefs are reproduced exactly as the study-guide application presented them in Section~\ref{app:eval-scenarios}.

\subsubsection*{Fixed Scenario Order}
\label{sec:eval-order}

Scenario~A preceded Scenario~B for every participant. This was a design decision, not an omission. The object of study is how a graph enters a workflow a student has already formed, so every participant had to meet the graph at the same point on the same learning curve; counterbalancing would have given half the cohort a workflow formed with the graph and half one formed without, and at $N=11$ the two halves ($n=5$ against $n=6$) could not have been compared. Greenwald's analysis supports the choice on both counts: counterbalancing distributes practice effects across conditions but does not remove an interaction between treatment and practice~\cite[p.~320]{greenwald1976within}, and where the course of learning is itself the object of study, a fixed repeated-measurement sequence is the appropriate design~\cite[pp.~316--318]{greenwald1976within}.

The cost was accepted knowingly. Graph availability and second exposure are perfectly collinear, so nothing in this chapter attributes a change in speed or in outcome to the graph. What the design buys is the one comparison it was built for, whether and where the graph displaces a surface of the established workflow, and Section~\ref{sec:eval-ab} confines its claims to that; the second-run effects the design cannot attribute are reported there separately and labelled as such.

\subsubsection*{Scope and Non-Goals}
\label{sec:eval-nongoals}

This study does not evaluate plan accuracy or generation speed, and does not use either to compare the two conditions. Establishing either would require matched cohorts with a known baseline of curriculum knowledge, a tool-agnostic operationalisation of plan quality, constant task exposure, and a sample large enough to isolate condition effects from individual variance, none of which a qualitative, small-scale design provides. The completion audit in Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness} is therefore not a performance comparison. It serves one measurement purpose: by establishing independently which plans met their brief, it makes visible the gap between what students reported achieving and what they achieved, and so fixes what the self-report instruments can be read to mean.

\subsection{System Under Test}
\label{sec:eval-system}

The system is the tool designed in Chapter~\ref{chap:design} and built in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, as it stood at the time of the sessions; it is not re-described here. Five of its components carry the findings and are named so that the codes below can be read against them: the Table View (Section~\ref{sec:table-view}), the Graph View with its filter sidebar and its togglable overlay of the prerequisite pairs the curriculum fixes (Section~\ref{sec:graph-spatial}), the Dashboard driven by the Compliance Engine (Sections~\ref{sec:dashboard} and~\ref{sec:compliance}), the Recommendation Panel (Section~\ref{sec:rec-panel}), and the Parking Stage (Section~\ref{sec:parking}). The Course Catalogue with free-text search (Section~\ref{sec:sidebar-design}), the visual legend (Section~\ref{sec:legend}), and the onboarding tour (Section~\ref{sec:onboarding}) were present and unchanged in both conditions.

\subsection{Procedure and Instruments}
\label{sec:eval-procedure}

Each session followed a fixed sequence administered through the purpose-built study-guide web application described in Section~\ref{sec:impl-userstudy}: (1)~demographics; (2)~recording consent; (3)~a short video tutorial of the tool; (4)~Scenario~A; (5)~the post-task questionnaire for Scenario~A; (6)~Scenario~B; (7)~the post-task questionnaire for Scenario~B; (8)~an open-exploration phase in which participants tried features freely; (9)~the final questionnaire; and (10)~a semi-structured voice interview. Sessions were screen- and audio-recorded. The think-aloud method was used throughout the working phases, that is in Scenario~A, in Scenario~B, and in the open-exploration phase, and a moderator was present to resolve operational questions about the interface.

After each scenario, participants completed an identical nine-item questionnaire: six items derived from the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), adapted to the planning domain~\cite{davis_perceived_1989}, three for perceived usefulness and three for perceived ease of use, followed by three items on task success, satisfaction with the plan, and task difficulty. After both scenarios and the open-exploration phase, participants completed the 26-item User Experience Questionnaire once~\cite{laugwitz_construction_2008}. Because the acceptance items are a domain-adapted short form rather than Davis's validated six-item scales, they are reported as TAM-derived throughout, and the reliabilities Davis reports for those scales ($\alpha = .98$ for usefulness, $.94$ for ease of use) do not transfer to them. The full instruments are reproduced in Section~\ref{app:eval-instruments}.

\subsection{Analysis Method}
\label{sec:eval-analysis}

Two independent records were produced per participant and then triangulated (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-pipeline}). The first is the think-aloud and interview transcript. The second is the screen recording, treated as an artefact in its own right: each recording was sampled at ten-second intervals and every sampled frame was labelled against a fixed scheme recording the active surface, the panels open, the sidebar contents, the per-semester ECTS read off the lane headers, the Dashboard state, and any transient banner text. This produced 2{,}179 labelled frames across the twenty-two scenario runs. The labels describe what is on screen rather than what it means, which is what makes them delegable: the author labelled 300 of the frames by hand, and the full set was labelled by a vision-language model run locally against the same scheme, so that no recording left the author's machine. The agreement between the two label sets on the 300 frames both labelled is reported with the scheme in Section~\ref{app:eval-stagelog}, and it is the check on which every count in this chapter rests. Interpretation was not delegated: the codes, the severities and the completion audit are the author's.

The two records exist because each of the three lenses of Research Question~3 needs its own. The planning process is read from the path through the interface, which surfaces were occupied, for how long and in what order, rather than from elapsed time, because one duration can cover a direct route to a complete plan or a long sequence of rejected placements; durations are reported only where they describe the shape of a session. The plans produced are read from whether they satisfy their brief and from the share of placements the tool rejected, not from whether students believed they had finished. Satisfaction, in the sense of ISO~9241-11~\cite{iso9241_11}, is read from the questionnaires and interviews, where self-report is the appropriate instrument. The records are needed together: Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness} shows that every participant reported success in a condition in which no plan met its brief, so self-report alone would have answered the effectiveness half of the question incorrectly.

Sessions were conducted in German; participant quotations in this chapter are the author's translations, and the German originals are held with the transcripts. Transcripts and recordings were analysed with the method of the formative study, codebook thematic analysis in the sense of Braun and Clarke~\cite{braun_thematic_2022}, and for the same reason: it yields frequencies that can be compared across a cohort while keeping every count traceable to the utterances and frames that carry it (Section~\ref{sec:analysis-method}). The two codebooks were built differently because the two studies ask different questions. The formative study had to discover needs, so its themes were built inductively from open codes (Section~\ref{sec:initial-coding}). This study observes a designed system whose problems, positive findings and neutral observations can be classified as they occur, so it starts from a controlled codebook of usability problems (\mbox{E-P}), positive findings (\mbox{E-G}), and neutral or method observations (\mbox{E-N}), extended only where an observation fitted no existing code. Each code is mapped to an ISO~9241-11 dimension and each problem carries a severity rating on Nielsen's five-point scale~\cite{nielsen1993}. As in the formative study, the final codebook was applied to every participant, so that a code coined while analysing a later session is also decided for the earlier ones and frequencies are comparable across the cohort rather than reflecting the order in which codes were introduced. Section~\ref{app:eval-matrix} reports the resulting matrix.

Frequencies are written as \emph{n}/\emph{m}, where \emph{m} is the number of participants for whom the code could be decided and \emph{n} the number for whom it was evidenced. The denominator is eleven for most codes and smaller where a code could not be decided for every participant, either because the relevant feature was never reached in that session or because the record does not settle it; a code reported as 9/9 was therefore evidenced in every session in which the question arose, not in nine of eleven. Severity is a single-analyst judgement, so the ranking in Table~\ref{tab:problems} is indicative rather than exact.

\section{The Planning Loop}
\label{sec:eval-process}

Planning under this tool is a single checklist-driven loop, and it has the same shape for every participant. Students read what the Dashboard says is still missing, find a candidate that closes the gap, place it, and return to the list. This section establishes that loop from the state logs; Section~\ref{sec:eval-ab} then asks what the graph changes in it, and Section~\ref{sec:eval-around} what the surfaces around it contribute.

\subsection{A State Model of the Planning Process}
\label{sec:eval-statemodel}

Each sampled frame carries a primary surface and, where that surface is the table, whether the Dashboard panel is open and whether the sidebar shows the Course Catalogue or the graph's filters. Combining these yields the process states of Table~\ref{tab:states}. Three quantities are derived from them, and each answers a different question. Occupancy, the share of sampled frames in a state, says where attention went. Entries, the number of times a state was entered, and dwell, the mean number of consecutive frames per entry expressed in seconds, separate one long visit from many short ones, which is what distinguishes a surface a student settles into from one they consult and leave. Self-transitions are removed before counting transitions, so a transition is a genuine change of state rather than a frame boundary.

The Recommendation Panel is not a state. It is a drawer that opens over the canvas without displacing the catalogue, the graph or the Dashboard, so a frame with the panel open is still in one of the six states; the panel is recorded per frame, given in Table~\ref{tab:occupancy} as an overlay, and analysed in Section~\ref{sec:eval-recs}.

\subsection{The Core Workflow: A Checklist-Driven Two-State Loop}
\label{sec:eval-loop}

The Dashboard's missing-requirements checklist was the completion mechanism for every participant (E-G15, 11/11). Students seeded the plan with the pre-filled mandatory backbone, which every participant accepted and valued (E-G01, 11/11), and from there read the remaining gap, acted to close it, and returned to the list. P09 opens Scenario~A by reading the count aloud, \enquote{22 missing requirements, wow, that is a lot}, and then works it down item by item; P10 names the driver directly, \enquote{I need narrow-elective modules, transferable skills, so let me see what narrow-elective ones there are}. For the most junior participant the loop did not begin until the moderator pointed at the tracker: P06 had been placing courses for roughly nine minutes with no view of what remained, and his reply marks the moment browsing turned into a loop (\enquote{exactly that, here I see what I need}). That the loop takes the same shape for fluent and for heavily assisted participants is what makes it a property of the tool rather than of facilitation.

The loop runs between two states: the working state, \textsc{dash+cat}, in which the checklist is read, and a candidate-finding state, which in Scenario~A is the catalogue. \textsc{cat}~$\rightarrow$~\textsc{dash+cat} accounts for 27.5\,\% of all state changes in Scenario~A and the reverse for 17.4\,\%, so the pair accounts for 44.9\,\% of everything that happens. The cycle is short: counting the states a participant passes through between one return to an open Dashboard and the next gives a median of 2.0 ($n=38$ returns), the minimum a two-state cycle can produce, which means that students went out, found one candidate, and came straight back to the checklist. What the graph does to this cycle is the subject of the next section.

\section{What the Graph Changes}
\label{sec:eval-ab}

The graph enters the planning loop at exactly one point: it replaces the catalogue as the surface at which a named gap is turned into candidate courses, and it leaves everything downstream of that untouched. This section reports the substitution, the division of labour it reveals between the two views, and the measure that bounds both, the rejection rate, which does not move. The second-run effects the fixed order confounds are collected at the end of the section and not attributed to the graph.

\subsection{Substitution: The Graph Replaces the Catalogue}
\label{sec:eval-substitution}

The graph does not add a third station to the planning cycle; it takes the place of one. In Scenario~B the cycle runs between the graph and the Dashboard, with \textsc{dash+cat}~$\rightarrow$~\textsc{graph} and its reverse at 19.3\,\% each, 38.6\,\% of all state changes together, where the catalogue--Dashboard cycle had accounted for 44.9\,\% in Scenario~A. Catalogue-only occupancy falls from 27.1\,\% of frames to 6.7\,\%, while the graph accounts for 26.6\,\%, nearly the share the catalogue vacates. The entry counts are closer still: the catalogue is entered 40 times across the eleven Scenario~A runs and the graph 42 times across the eleven Scenario~B runs, at comparable mean dwell (81~s against 62~s). From the working state, the probability of moving to the catalogue falls from 3.1\,\% in Scenario~A to 0.0\,\% in Scenario~B, while the probability of moving to the graph is 6.4\,\%. Per participant, catalogue occupancy falls in ten of eleven cases, from a median of 27.4\,\% to 4.5\,\%. Figure~\ref{fig:eval-loop} renders both cycles.

The cycle keeps its period. The median number of states between two returns to the checklist is 2.0 in Scenario~B ($n=48$ returns), as it was in Scenario~A: adding a third surface did not lengthen the excursion between two consultations of the checklist. What changed is only which surface the excursion goes to.

This is the comparison the two conditions were run for, and it is one the fixed order does not touch. Practice alone does not explain abandoning a catalogue used competently in the first run for a filter panel that did not exist in it; the substitution is a consequence of graph availability, and it is the only comparison in this chapter of which that can be said.

\subsection{Query Support Without Commit Support}
\label{sec:eval-graphrole}

The substitution locates the graph at one step of the loop, and the recordings show why it stops there. The graph supports the query step, in which a named gap is turned into a set of candidate courses, and it does not support the commit step, in which a candidate is assigned to a semester. This is the division of labour the design assigned in advance, exploration to the graph and commitment to the table (Section~\ref{sec:mutual-exclusion}), here observed rather than assumed; and the reason it holds is a consequence of what each surface displays, not a preference participants held.

The query step is where the graph is used well. The clearest instance is P02 in Scenario~B: he expands the missing-requirements panel, reads that seven narrow-elective modules are required and two are present, switches to the graph, narrows the obligation filter to narrow electives, pans the resulting clusters harvesting candidates into the Parking Stage, returns to the table, and places them.
P09 found a sharper variant unaided, combining the term-availability filter with the \enquote{not planned} course-state filter so that the graph displays exactly the outstanding set, and read a definitive negative answer off it (\enquote{everything offered in winter and summer would already be planned}); P12 formed the same combination for winter courses he had not yet planned and placed twelve credits from what it returned (E-G18). P09 switched into the graph for the single purpose of finding a course of a given ECTS value, a query the catalogue cannot express: \enquote{for that I switch to the graph view, I see it immediately with my filters}.

The commit step returns to the table for a structural reason. Plan state did change inside the graph, in both cohorts and for participants across the experience range, so the graph is not a read-only inspector; P12 placed courses from graph nodes in all three of his excursions. What the graph does not render, at any zoom level, is the semester dimension: neither lanes nor per-lane ECTS totals appear anywhere in Graph View (E-P47, evidenced for ten of the ten participants for whom it can be decided). A student placing a course from a node therefore cannot see which semester is being loaded.
P10 names the missing information directly (\enquote{adding directly from here I would probably not do, because I do not know which semester has room}), and when he does place from a node moments later it succeeds only because he remembers which lane had room (\enquote{I could still recall that I wanted to do something there anyway}), and P12 states the consequence (\enquote{I do not even know how much I have, I have to switch over again}). P06 provides the demonstration: a course placed from a graph node pushed a lane past the nine-ECTS exchange cap, and the breach became visible only after returning to the table. A second view can therefore be committed from only if it carries enough of the plan's state to show the consequence of the commitment.

\subsection{What Does Not Change, and What Cannot Be Attributed}
\label{sec:eval-accuracy}

Placement accuracy does not change, and this is the measure that bounds the substitution. The share of placement attempts ending in a rejection moves from a median of 16.7\,\% to 13.6\,\%, but it falls in only seven of eleven participants and rises in four, a split consistent with chance (sign test, $p = 0.55$). Rejections per ten minutes in fact rise, from 1.2 to 1.8, because placement activity rises. The graph changed \emph{where candidates were found} and left \emph{how placements were committed} untouched. This follows from what the surfaces display: the rework loop at the commit stage arises from term parity and per-lane load not being shown on the semester lane (Section~\ref{sec:eval-friction}), which is a property of the table and not of the graph.

\label{sec:eval-rate}
Two further differences between the runs are recorded here and attributed to nothing, because graph availability and second exposure are collinear (Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}). Placements per ten minutes rise from a median of 7.8 in Scenario~A to 13.9 in Scenario~B, in ten of eleven participants, while total sampled time falls from 20.8 to 13.3 median minutes on a task carrying one additional semester; and all six of the runs that met every constraint of their brief are Scenario~B runs (Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}). A participant who is faster or more compliant the second time may be so because of the filters, because the interface is familiar, because the curriculum is familiar, or because the brief is familiar, and this design distinguishes none of these. \section{Around the Loop: Staging, Recommending, Configuring}
\label{sec:eval-around}

Three surfaces sit beside the loop without being stations of it. The Parking Stage is what lets the two views work on one plan; the Recommendation Panel accompanies the loop without driving it; and the compliance thresholds the loop is checked against were set by the students themselves. Each is reported with what it contributed and what it cost.

\subsection{The Parking Stage: Shortlisting Before Commitment}
\label{sec:eval-parking}

The Parking Stage is what allows two surfaces to operate on one plan without either having to do the other's work, and every participant used it to shortlist before committing (E-G06, 11/11). In the graph-on condition it is where courses discovered in the graph were held before assignment. P04 described the strategy in advance (\enquote{I will put everything into the parking zone and then go back to the table and assign each to the right semester}), and the recordings show it executed: P09 moved nine courses from graph nodes into the Parking Stage in a single episode, and P10 nine across two.

That the stage sits outside the plan's accounting is both its function and a source of error. Parked credits are excluded from the planned ECTS total and nothing on screen says so (E-P38, 10/11). P02 planned against a total of 102~ECTS while 24~ECTS sat parked; P10 and P12 each ended a run with 6~ECTS parked while the Dashboard reported a complete 180~ECTS plan and zero missing requirements. Nine participants asked, in one form or another, for the stage to be visible from inside the graph, where it is not (E-P37, 9/11).

\subsection{The Recommendation Panel Accompanies the Loop Rather Than Driving It}
\label{sec:eval-recs}

The Recommendation Panel was one of the most occupied surfaces in the tool, and almost every participant opened it, but it did not become a station of the planning cycle. It was open for 24\,\% of sampled frames in Scenario~A and 20\,\% in Scenario~B, which is the same order as the catalogue in Scenario~A (27.1\,\%) and the graph in Scenario~B (26.6\,\%), and ten of the eleven participants opened it at least once. Both records agree on which participant did not: the one blank cell in E-G02 (10/11) is the same participant whose frames never show the panel.

Occupancy overstates consultation, however, and the block structure of the record says why. The panel was entered 23 times in Scenario~A and eight times in Scenario~B across all eleven runs, against 40 catalogue entries and 41 graph entries, and 71\,\% of the time it was open falls inside nine episodes longer than three minutes. The longest is a single unbroken episode of nearly fifteen minutes in P01's Scenario~A, entered once and never closed. The panel was therefore frequently present rather than frequently consulted, and its high occupancy records a drawer left open beside the working state rather than a surface students returned to in the way they returned to the checklist.

What students did with it divides along their confidence in the curriculum. Recommendations were actively used and dragged into the plan by ten of eleven (E-G02), and seven participants saw the panel re-rank to an interest they had just typed and recognised that it had (E-G14, 7/11). Against that, seven participants browsed the recommendations and then set them aside in favour of the explicit checklist and catalogue search (E-N05, 7/11), and six of those seven are among the ten who had used recommendations earlier in the same session, so this is a considered rejection rather than a failure to find the feature. The two participants who could not yet judge modules for themselves leaned on the panel instead, using it and the Dashboard as a substitute for curriculum knowledge (E-N06, 2/11). The condition data show the same split more sharply: in Scenario~B five participants never opened the panel at all, while three kept it open for more than half the run.

Three defects bound what these figures can be read to mean. The panel is a drawer over the canvas, and for six participants it overlapped and occluded the semester lanes it was meant to feed (E-P11, 6/11), which is a direct cost of the coexistence that keeps it out of the state model. Where a recommendation family was consulted for a specific gap it sometimes returned nothing: the internships tab rendered a bare empty state in a task that required an internship semester (E-P42, 4/11), and P09 met it while looking for exactly that. And although every card carries a reason, four of the nine participants for whom it can be decided could not tell which of their own profile inputs had produced a given suggestion (E-P68, 4/9), so the explanation identified the relationship but not its origin.

\subsection{User-Configured Compliance Thresholds}
\label{sec:eval-config}

The rule-checker students trusted as their measure of completion was one they had configured themselves, and they trusted it whether or not they had configured it correctly. The profile exposes a maximum and a recommended ECTS value per semester, the first-use flow shows where, and aligning them to the brief was part of the task as set. Neither scenario band is expressible as a band, and the default is 30~ECTS. Participants who aligned the profile to their brief obtained an engine that enforced the task; those who did not were checked against the default, and nothing told them so.

The recordings show both outcomes. P01 made the change within four minutes of first contact and discovered the asymmetry in the same breath (\enquote{I will set it to 33 straight away, more is not allowed, and is there a minimum? No}). P10 set 33 and 30 within three minutes. P12 narrated the mismatch in Scenario~B before correcting it by hand. P11 changed the recommended value from 30 to 24 midway through a run, which moved what the tool counted as a violation partway through the session. Where the profile was not aligned, the tool checked the wrong thing: P06 completed Scenario~B with one semester at 30~ECTS against a stated band of 21 to 27 while the Dashboard reported no warning, because its target was still 30 (E-P32, 11/11; E-P57, 11/11).

A second asymmetry is visible across all twenty-two runs: the upper bound blocks and the lower bound is silent (E-P35, 10/11). Every warning the tool emits has the form \enquote{Semester~N is heavy}. No lane below the band floor produced one, including P05-A at 12~ECTS, P03-B at 8, P09-A at 12, P11-B at 20, and P12-B at 12. That asymmetry, rather than buried status, accounts for most of the surviving band violations reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}.

\subsection{Where the Loop Breaks}
\label{sec:eval-friction}

The most costly problems cluster at three stages of the loop rather than spreading evenly across the interface, and each stage fails in a different way: the drive stage does not show the status the loop depends on, the find stage cannot be narrowed to the gap being closed, and the commit stage withholds the fact needed to place a course correctly.

At the drive stage the status the loop depends on was not glanceable. Requirement and ECTS-gap status lived behind collapsible Dashboard panels (E-P10, 11/11), and where status was shown it was written as prose rather than given as a figure (E-P03, 7/11; P01: \enquote{how many do I have? I do not see it}). A requirement named in the Dashboard's missing list could not be followed to the courses that satisfy it, so the module had to be re-found by hand in the catalogue (E-P58); all four of the most recent participants demonstrate the round trip, reading a module name out of the panel and retyping it into search. The warning list is the extreme case: across the 437 frames of the four most recent sessions the warnings panel was never opened once, while its counter climbed to between two and five in every run.

At the find stage the catalogue could not be narrowed. Filtering by category, obligation, or ECTS was available only in the graph's sidebar (E-P04, 11/11, severity~3), so students in the graph-off condition fell back on free-text search. P10 states the fallback and its cost in one line: \enquote{that does not work, so I have to pick them out by hand}. The Recommendation Panel was the other route to a candidate, and Section~\ref{sec:eval-recs} reports both how far it carried students and where it returned nothing.

At the commit stage the tool withheld the fact needed to place a course correctly. A course's winter or summer availability is not shown on the lanes, so students inferred a term, were rejected, and re-placed (E-P29, 11/11). This is the study's clearest rework loop, and it is a display gap rather than a rule problem: the rule is correct and the message is prompt, but the information needed to avoid the rejection is absent at the moment of the decision. Ten participants asked for a term indicator on the lane, most of them unprompted, and several audibly counted parity aloud in its place.

\section{The Plans Produced, Against Self-Report}
\label{sec:eval-effectiveness}

Every participant reported success in a condition in which no plan met its brief. All eleven rated their Scenario~A outcome as \enquote{Yes}, and not one Scenario~A run satisfied the stated constraints. Self-reported success in this study therefore measures felt rather than actual completion (E-N03, 9/11), and the observational record rather than the questionnaire carries the claim about what was produced. This is the reason the completion audit was conducted at all (Section~\ref{sec:eval-nongoals}), and it is the section's principal result.

The brief's constraints divide into two kinds, and the plans have to be read against each separately. The tool can represent the ECTS total, the curriculum's own requirements and a per-semester ceiling, the last through the profile a student sets; it cannot represent a lower bound on a semester's load or a reduced-load semester for an internship or exchange, because it has no minimum and no semester type. Against the constraints it can represent, seventeen of the twenty-one resolved runs are clean; the four that are not are two runs that ended short of the total (P01) and two with semesters above the ceiling planned against a profile still at its default (P03-B, P06-B), which is the configuration failure of Section~\ref{sec:eval-config}. Against the full brief, six of the twenty-two runs met every stated constraint, most of the remainder failed on one or two semesters, and one run is left unresolved because two lane values were never legible; the per-run audit is in Section~\ref{app:eval-completion}. The difference between seventeen and six is the two constraints the tool cannot hold: the reduced-load semester and the band's floor were handled by the students themselves, with mixed results and with no confirmation from the tool either way (E-P25 and E-P26, both 11/11). All six fully compliant runs are Scenario~B runs and Scenario~A produced none, which is the strongest single pattern in the completion data and is entirely confounded with order (Section~\ref{sec:eval-rate}).

The tool's own completion signal ran ahead of the observed state in the same way the self-reports did. All twenty-two runs ended with the Dashboard reporting zero missing requirements and a completed focus-area checklist, including a run with four lanes outside the stated band and no internship semester at all, and two runs with credits left in the Parking Stage. The calibration gap did not track experience: some participants over-rated, one was well calibrated, and an experienced participant under-rated a near-complete plan, so we report no consistent relationship between experience and calibration in this sample.

What the checklist did at the boundary of its own model is the sharpest instance of the same mechanism. P02 created an empty semester lane specifically to serve as the internship semester, then filled it to twelve ECTS because the Dashboard reported that six ECTS were still missing from the total and said nothing about the internship. The requirement he had represented himself was invisible to the mechanism he was following, so following the mechanism removed it. This is the study's central design implication, developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}: a checklist that students trust as their compass must cover every constraint they are held to, because the constraints it omits are the ones that will be optimised away.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}
Eleven students planned a Bachelor curriculum twice, once without the curriculum graph and once with it. The two records support five conclusions about the planning process and one about how the tool was received. Each is taken up in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.

Planning under this tool is a single checklist-driven loop. Students read what is missing, find a candidate, hold it, place it, and re-read. The loop was the completion mechanism for every participant, its two-state cycle accounts for roughly 40\,\% of all observed state changes in both conditions, and its period is the same with and without the graph.

The graph enters that loop at one point. It replaces the catalogue as the station at which a named gap is turned into candidate courses, taking almost exactly the occupancy and entry count the catalogue vacates, and it does not alter the rate at which placements are rejected. Its contribution is to the query step and not to the commit step, which is the division the design assigned in advance, and the reason it holds is structural: the graph renders no semester dimension, so the consequence of a placement made there is not visible from there.

What the graph makes legible is curriculum structure, and only the thinnest slice of sequence. Containment edges are drawn and valued, and the two prerequisite pairs the Bachelor curriculum fixes are drawn with them; the Compliance Engine reports those same two pairs as advisories when they are planned out of order, and one such advisory fired across the twenty-two runs. No other ordering information existed anywhere in the evaluated tool, in a view or in the engine. Two participants read the graph as making dependencies legible (E-G08), while four asked for ordering information at the point of placement and did not obtain it (E-P44). This evaluation therefore bounds its claim to the contribution of a browsable structural view carrying a near-empty prerequisite overlay, and cannot speak to what a view of recommended sequence would contribute, because none was on screen. This is where the second assumption of Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions}, that prerequisite sequencing is the planning-relevant structure a curriculum encodes, meets curricula that publish almost none; Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-related}) draws the consequence. On the first assumption, the hybrid concept itself, the findings are narrower: participants used the two surfaces for different steps and seven of eleven articulated that division as the value of having both (E-N01, 7/11), but no non-hybrid alternative was tested, so whether one surface could have served both steps remains open.

Recommendations accompanied the loop without becoming part of it. The panel was open for about a fifth to a quarter of all sampled frames and ten of eleven participants used it, but it was entered a fraction as often as the catalogue or the graph, and seven participants browsed it and then returned to the checklist and search to decide. Its value divided by curriculum confidence: the participants who could not yet judge modules leaned on it, and those who could set it aside.

The tool was received well, and the questionnaires say where its weaknesses are rather than contradicting the process account. Every UEQ scale is positive and pragmatic quality (+2.28) leads hedonic (+2.00), so students experienced a capable instrument before an engaging one; the two weakest scales, perspicuity (+1.82) and novelty (+1.77), correspond to the terminology and learnability problems the observational record shows and to the fact that experienced participants judged the tool against spreadsheets they already knew.
