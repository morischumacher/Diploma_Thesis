\label{chap:discussion}
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} reported participant behaviour and feedback regarding the evaluated artefact. This chapter abstracts from these individual sessions to evaluate the underlying design principles and connect the findings to existing literature. Specifically, it examines the validity of the initial design assumptions, interprets what the findings mean for the tool and for the process they describe, articulates the implications for hybrid planning interfaces, and situates the results within the prior work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. It then delineates the methodological limitations bounding the findings and asks how far the artefact and the findings carry beyond this study. The formal answers to the research questions and directions for future work are reserved for Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}. 
Following the nested model's premise that downstream validation can expose upstream flaws, it also reads those findings back onto the levels above, including the level~1 assumption that a combined graph-and-table interface is the right approach (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, Section~\ref{sec:paradigm}).
Ultimately, the analysis presented here is exploratory. Rather than declaring final laws of user behaviour, it synthesizes the observed sessions into grounded hypotheses, offering a foundation for larger-scale studies to test and refine.

\section{The Design's Assumptions, Revisited}
\label{sec:disc-assumptions}

The design of Chapter~\ref{chap:design} rested on assumptions it could not test, some inherited from the formative study and some made as design judgements. This section states, for each, what the evaluation now says about it.

\paragraph{Graph for exploration, table for commitment.}
Supported, but narrower in scope. The graph served the query step and the table the commit step for every participant who used both (Section~\ref{sec:eval-graphrole}), but what students did in the graph was directed querying, turning a named gap into a candidate set, rather than the open-ended browsing the formative study called exploration (Section~\ref{sec:mutual-exclusion}).

\paragraph{A staging area between finding and committing.}
Held, and more important than the design claimed. Every participant shortlisted before committing (E-G06, 11/11), and the Parking Stage became the mechanism by which work found in one view reached the other; its cost, parked credits invisible to every completion readout (E-P38, 10/11), was not anticipated.

\paragraph{The Dashboard as the student's compass.}
Held beyond its intention. The missing-requirements list drove the planning process for every participant (E-G15, 11/11), and because participants met the curriculum only through it (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}), what it did not hold them to did not exist for them. Two constraints of the briefs show this. The reduced-load semester, the one it cannot model at all, is the unmet constraint in half of the runs that missed their brief (Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}); the per-semester load it can model, but its thresholds live in the student's profile, so a plan was held to the brief only by the participants who had set them to it (Section~\ref{sec:eval-config}).

\paragraph{Guide rather than block.}
Held for the rules it covered (E-G04, 11/11), with one asymmetry the design did not intend. The same workload band whose thresholds the student supplies is also split across the two tiers: exceeding the ceiling is blocked, while falling below the floor raises only a dismissible warning, so an under-loaded semester is never forced to correct (E-P35, 10/11).

\paragraph{Recommendations with reasons, beside the plan.}
Half held. Reasoned suggestions were read and used by ten of eleven participants, but they did not drive the process: those who could judge the curriculum for themselves browsed and returned to the checklist (E-N05, 7/11), and the reasons named a relationship without naming the input that had produced it (E-P68, 4/9).

\paragraph{An overlay that draws only what the curriculum fixes.}
Sound as a rule, and empty in application. An edge should carry a consequence, which is what keeps an overlay legible; the Bachelor programme the study planned in fixes no ordering between two named courses at all, the two it publishes being advisory, which the rule excluded, so the overlay in front of participants was empty rather than merely sparse, and four of them asked for ordering information at the point of placement (E-P44, 4/11).

\paragraph{The two upstream assumptions.}
On the hybrid concept (Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions}), the evaluation says only that the two surfaces served different steps of one loop and that seven participants named that division as the value of having both (E-N01, 7/11); no non-hybrid alternative was run, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}) states the open question. On what a curriculum encodes, the finding is the curriculum's rather than the evaluation's: the regulations in scope publish almost no sequencing, and students used the graph for what such a curriculum affords, orientation and filtered browsing.

\section{Interpretation of Findings}
\label{sec:disc-interpretation}

\paragraph{The primary result is a process, not a feature.}
The primary answer to RQ3 is that participants converged on one stable loop, and that this loop, rather than any single component, is what their sessions were organised around. The pre-fill seeds a plan, the checklist drives it, the recommendations, the catalogue and the graph propose candidates, the Parking Stage holds them, and the table commits them. The loop is the same for the fluent, unassisted participants and for the most junior participant who had to be shown where it started, so it is a property of the artefact rather than of facilitation. It is measurable: its two-station cycle accounts for 44.9~\% of all observed state changes in Scenario~A and 38.6~\% in Scenario~B, and its period is unchanged by the graph. And it externalises the part of programme planning students cannot hold in their heads, which requirements remain open and what would close them, so that attention goes to choosing rather than to tracking.

\paragraph{The tool was the curriculum.}
Because the briefs stated no curriculum rules, participants met them only through the Dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}). For the duration of a task the tool did not support an understanding of the curriculum; it constituted it. This is the general form of several specific findings: terminology the tool did not define stayed unclear (E-P01), a requirement the tool did not model was optimised away, and the two least confident participants used the Dashboard and the recommendations as a substitute for curriculum knowledge (E-N06). A tool that is the student's only channel to the rules inherits the obligation to be complete, which is stronger than the obligation to be correct.

\paragraph{The Parking Stage is the mechanism that makes a two-view plan coherent.}
A hybrid interface over one plan has to move work between views without either view duplicating the other. The Parking Stage is how this tool does it: a buffer outside the plan's accounting where courses found in the graph, or surfaced by a recommendation, wait until they are assigned in the table, and every participant used it that way. This is offered as a hypothesis for other shared-plan hybrids: that a shared-plan hybrid does not need each view to do everything, but does need an explicit place for a decision made and not yet committed. The same property is its cost, since parked credits are invisible to every completion readout and several participants compensated for that in their heads.

\paragraph{The graph occupies a place in the process rather than adding one.}
When the graph becomes available it does not extend the planning cycle with a third station; it takes over the station the catalogue held, at the catalogue's occupancy and entry count, and the cycle keeps its shape and its period (Section~\ref{sec:eval-substitution}). What changes is the surface on which a named requirement is turned into candidate courses. The contribution of a hybrid structural view, in this tool and this curriculum, is to the query step of planning and not to the commit step. The fixed order scopes it, since the graph was met only after a workflow had formed without it, so the claim is about a structural view entering an established workflow rather than about one met from the start (Section~\ref{sec:disc-limitations}). Section~\ref{sec:disc-related} reads the seam through cognitive fit.

\paragraph{Where the graph's value sat: the query step, and largely its filters.}
The participants who read the graph most fluently attributed what they gained in the graph-on condition to the filter sidebar rather than to the node--link drawing (E-G07, 11/11; E-P04, 11/11), and the recordings agree: participants entered a view several of them found hard to read in order to reach a control they found essential, and one switched into the graph for the single purpose of finding a course of a given ECTS value, a query the catalogue cannot express. The value of the graph-on condition therefore sits largely in a component separable from the visualisation. The graph is not thereby worthless, since interest-driven browsing (E-G10, 9/11) and module composition (E-G17, 6/11) are things the filters do not give; but the study cannot tell how much of its contribution would survive if the table had the filters, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}) states that as the hypothesis it is.

\paragraph{Placement returns to the table because of what the graph does not display.}
Plan changes were made inside the graph by participants across the experience range, so the graph is not a read-only inspector. The asymmetry that holds is narrower: semester lanes and their totals are not rendered in Graph View at any zoom level, so a student placing from a node cannot see the load consequence of the placement. One participant breached a reduced-load cap this way and discovered it only after switching back, and two named the missing information as their reason for not placing from the graph at all. Stated generally, a second view can be committed from only if it carries enough of the plan's state to show the consequence of the commitment.

\paragraph{A user-supplied compliance layer checks the wrong thing for the users who do not supply it.}
Most participants set the profile's workload thresholds to their brief, and the engine then enforced what they had set. Those who did not were checked against the defaults, and two Scenario~B runs ended with a semester above the brief's ceiling and no warning, because the ceiling in force was still the default (Section~\ref{sec:eval-config}). The setting is discoverable and it works; the consequence is structural rather than about particular students. A rule-checker whose thresholds come from the user is correct only for those who supply them, and it reports the same confident verdict either way, so a plan checked against the wrong band is indistinguishable from one checked against the right one. Which students are at risk is not something eleven sessions can establish: both unconfigured runs are by participants who reported no prior experience with study-planning tools, but four other participants who reported none did set the profile. The asymmetry between a blocking ceiling and an advisory floor compounds the problem, since an under-filled semester is never asked to correct.
\paragraph{Explainable recommendations are scaffolding whose value scales inversely with domain confidence.}
Participants who could not yet judge the curriculum leaned on the recommendations as a substitute for domain knowledge, while those who knew it browsed and then declined them in favour of the checklist and catalogue search, although the engine visibly responded to their stated interests. The pattern suggests that an explainable recommendation is worth most to the least expert user and that confident users want a different route to the same end; its design consequence is stated below.

\paragraph{Self-report measured felt completion, and the checklist's boundary is where plans failed.}
Seven runs were rated a success by the participant who produced them and did not meet their brief, and all twenty-two ended with the Dashboard reporting no missing requirements, a report that was correct for the curriculum's requirements and silent on the task's (Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}). A student can hold a correct model of the task and lose it, because the model and the checklist are never weighed against each other: the checklist speaks in the interface, continuously and with authority, while the student's own constraint has no representation there and has to be remembered against it. When the two conflict the checklist wins, not because students trust it more than themselves but because it is the only one of the two being read, and a constraint outside its model is traded away in satisfying the constraints inside it. The lesson is not that students over-report; it is that a checklist students treat as their compass must cover every constraint they are held to.

\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:disc-implications}
Each implication states a rule for interfaces that guide a user through a constrained, multi-step decision, followed by the observation in this study that produced it. They are written to hold beyond this artefact, but they are untested outside it and outside this sample; the study evidence is what recommends them, not what establishes them.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A signal users treat as authoritative must model everything they are accountable for.} A partial completion signal is not read as partial. Here the checklist drove every session and reported no missing requirements in all twenty-two runs while eight missed their brief, because it modelled the curriculum's rules and not the task's; parked credits sat outside the totals it reported (E-P38, 10/11); and its thresholds came from the user, so an unconfigured checker validated against the wrong band. Such a signal should cover every constraint the user is held to, count or visibly exclude what is staged, and derive its thresholds rather than assume them.
    \item \textbf{Put the information a decision needs in the surface where the decision is made.} Separating a decision from its evidence costs a round trip, and users take that round trip only while they remember it is needed. Filters existed beside the graph while placement happened in the table (E-P04, 11/11); requirement status sat behind collapsible panels, and a warning counter that climbed to five was never opened in four of eleven sessions (E-P10, 11/11).
    \item \textbf{A view may be committed from only if it shows the consequence of committing.} The Graph View renders no semester dimension (E-P47), so a placement made there hides the load it creates, and one participant breached a cap without seeing it. This is what confines the graph to the query step, and it is a property of what a view displays rather than of the representation it uses.
    \item \textbf{Feedback that governs an action should arrive before the action, not after it.} The tool computes the legal set for the placement menu but does not mark legal targets during a drag (E-P05, 11/11), and it holds a course's term but does not show it on the lane (E-P29, 11/11); together these produce a rework loop, in which users infer, are rejected, and re-place.
    \item \textbf{Serve the guided and the self-directed route equally.} Recommendations carried the participants without a curriculum model of their own, while those who had one returned to the checklist and a filtered search (E-N05, 7/11). Neither route is a fallback for the other, and a tool that invests in one leaves the other's users unserved.
\end{itemize}

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings are consistent with, qualify and extend the work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. The three are taken in turn, and the extension is developed over the four paragraphs that follow it: the dashboard critique, the integration this tool was built to test, and the theory that accounts for what the graph did.
First, the results are \emph{consistent with} the specific usability friction points identified by Bartel et al.~\cite{bartel_design_2024} regarding study-planning assistants. In a different interface and sample, participants in this study met the same two difficulties, reading institutional curriculum terminology and finding nested filter controls. Design principles name known hazards without conferring immunity to them.

Second, the findings \emph{qualify} the foundational premise of the graph-visualisation literature. Systems such as \emph{ViCurriAS}~\cite{zucker_vicurrias_2009}, \emph{STOPS}~\cite{auvinen_stops_2014}, and the elective visualisations by Trippel and Röpke~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} rest on the premise that a curriculum carries a prerequisite network worth drawing. The curricula in this study carry little of one. Outside the introductory phase the regulations state few dependencies, so the implemented graph draws containment rather than sequence, and a prerequisite advisory arose once across the twenty-two runs. The observation suggests, for curricula that encode few hard dependencies, that the value of a curriculum graph shifts from showing sequence to supporting orientation and filtered browsing.

Third, the results \emph{extend} the AIStudyBuddy lineage~\cite{roepke_study_2024, judel_supporting_2023, trippel_developing_2025} in two distinct directions. First, the finding that a graph-based curriculum visualisation is well received~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} is reproduced here at the level of reception and qualified at the level of use: the graph served as a query surface that displaced the catalogue rather than as a planning surface, and the participants who read it most fluently judged it substitutable by its own filter panel. Furthermore, where prior work separates student applications from institution-facing analytics~\cite{roepke_study_2024}, this study contributes a student-side, in-workflow account that institutional metrics do not reach. Specifically, it records how the completion signal and the recommendations are used while a plan is being formed, including the gap between reported and audited completion.

This gap gives the dashboard critique of Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} a counterpart at the level of the individual student. Caulfield's objection to Course Signals~\cite{caulfield2013} was that its retention result was read as an outcome the signal had produced when the analysis had not established that it had: the signal was credited beyond what had been checked. The completion finding here has the same shape at a smaller scale. The Dashboard reported no missing requirements, correctly for the curriculum's rules and silently on the task's, and participants read that report as completion in preference to their own model of the task. Parasuraman and Riley~\cite{parasuraman1997humans} describe the general hazard as misuse of automation, over-reliance that produces failures of monitoring or decision biases, and name the saliency of a system's state indicators among the factors that govern it. A completion checklist is an automation of that kind: continuous, salient and authoritative, and silent about what it has not checked, so a student who trusts it has no cue that anything remains to be monitored. The parallel is not a claim about Course Signals, whose signal and setting differ; it is that a trusted signal invites the same over-reliance whatever it measures, and that the remedy is a signal that covers the task rather than one users trust less.

These patterns also speak to the integration Bodily and Verbert asked for. Of the 93 articles in their review, 9\,\% describe an enhanced visualisation with recommendations and 6\,\% a visualisation with recommendations and data mining. Counted more broadly, as any article pairing a recommendations component with a visualisation or text feedback, 17\,\% qualify, and they call for interfaces that address both \emph{what} to do and \emph{why}~\cite{bodily_review_2017}. This tool provides both components, and the frame record shows them used together but not as one. The Dashboard drove the planning loop for every participant (E-G15, 11/11); the Recommendation Panel accompanied it, used by ten of eleven (E-G02, 10/11) but entered a fraction as often as the catalogue, and set aside by seven participants in favour of the checklist and search once they could judge the curriculum for themselves (E-N05, 7/11). The combination is used, with a division of labour that follows curriculum confidence; whether it works better than either component alone is not something this design can test.

The division has a shape the frame record makes precise, and reading it needed a tool carrying every component at once. The cycle turns on the checklist and one candidate-finding surface, while three parts of the tool sit beside it rather than in it (Section~\ref{sec:eval-around}). The Recommendation Panel is the clearest of the three, because its absence from the cycle is not an absence from the screen: it was open for 24\,\% of sampled frames in Scenario~A and 20\,\% in Scenario~B, shares comparable to the catalogue's 27.1\,\% and the graph's 26.6\,\%, yet 71\,\% of that time fell in nine episodes longer than three minutes, left open beside the work rather than navigated to. Pairing a dashboard with a recommender therefore produced neither one component nor two equal ones, but a driver and an accompaniment. Which of the two a student leant on followed their curriculum confidence, and the distinction is legible only where both are present in one workflow and the workflow itself is recorded.

Finally, the displacement of the catalogue by the graph is an instance of cognitive fit~\cite{vessey1991cognitive}. Vessey's theory holds that problem-solving performance depends on the match between representation and task: spatial tasks, those that require perceiving relationships, are served by graphs, while symbolic tasks, those that require extracting or acting on discrete values, are served by tables. The hybrid interface makes that distinction physical. Turning an open requirement into a candidate set is a task of relationships, and participants did it in the graph; committing a candidate to a semester is a task of discrete values, lane capacity and term, and participants did it in the table. The theory predicts the seam and the study observed it. What the theory does not predict, and the study adds, is that fit can be defeated by what a view omits: the table fits the commit task because it shows the semester dimension, which the graph does not.



\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}
The evaluation took one direction through a tool that admits many, so the limitations below bound this study design rather than the artefact. Most are the price of a decision made on purpose, to trace a small cohort frame by frame rather than survey a larger one. These boundaries dictate how the findings may be interpreted; unexplored research directions and alternative study designs are reserved for future work in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}).

\paragraph{Study design.}
The fixed order precludes causal claims about the graph. The two scenarios differ simultaneously in graph availability, persona context, task brief, and presentation order (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}). Therefore, graph availability and second-task exposure are collinear. Of the reported process measures, only the catalogue's displacement is attributed to the graph (Section~\ref{sec:eval-substitution}); the rise in placement rate is reported as a description of the second run. The fixed design also shapes the graph finding: because the graph was introduced only after a tabular workflow had formed, the observation that it functions as an excursion from an established loop is partially an artefact of the study design. The claim is scoped accordingly, here and where it is made (Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation}): when a structural view is introduced into a practised tabular workflow, it serves querying rather than committing. While this arguably reflects high ecological validity (students plausibly meet advanced features in sequence), the behaviour of a user who meets the graph first constitutes a separate condition reserved for future research.

\paragraph{What the design inherited from the formative study.}
The artefact under evaluation is one design among the many the requirements of Section~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements} admit, built from six interviews at one faculty and from design judgements the formative study could not adjudicate (Section~\ref{sec:disc-assumptions}). The evaluation therefore tests this realisation of those requirements, and cannot separate what the findings say about hybrid planning in general from what they say about the choices made here: giving the centre panel to one view at a time, scoping the overlay to stated prerequisites, or leaving the reduced-load semester unmodelled. Where a finding turns on such a choice it is stated as a property of the artefact, and the requirements themselves remain untested against an alternative elicitation.

\paragraph{Sample.}
Eleven sessions from two computing programmes at one institution bound generalisation, and bear on the graph findings in particular: computing students are plausibly more fluent with node--link representations than a general student population, which would if anything find the graph harder, so the more transferable part of the graph-on condition may be its filter panel rather than its drawing. Patterns resting on subsets of the sample, such as the expertise-moderated use of recommendations or the split reception of the graph, rest on few and in places single-participant contrasts with unmeasured moderators.

\paragraph{Ecological validity.}
The author designed and built the tool, moderated the sessions and coded them afterwards, and participants knew this, so self-report is exposed to demand characteristics; this is one reason the questionnaire means sit near the ceiling of both instruments and are read as the weakest evidence in the chapter. A master's thesis cannot separate the roles, and the frame record is the compensation, since what a participant did on screen is less exposed to that pressure than what they reported. The moderator also resolved operational and conceptual difficulties and unblocked several sessions, so task outcomes are facilitator-qualified rather than independent. Planning a persona's degree under a time limit is also not planning one's own, and the transfer of these loops to unprompted use is an open question.

\paragraph{Instrumentation and analysis.}
The acceptance items are TAM-derived rather than the validated TAM: three items per construct rather than six, reworded to the planning domain and answered on an agreement scale, with one ease-of-use item that measures a task outcome and is reported separately for that reason (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-questionnaire}); no reliability claim is made for the shortened scales. In the frame analysis, ten-second sampling misses anything shorter, so counts of transient banners are lower bounds, and a frame shows interface state rather than intent. The two recording cohorts differ in length and resolution, so a frequency that falls as a proportion across the cohort may reflect session duration rather than the interface; the difference and what it costs are set out with the method in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:eval-framemethod}). Finally, the analysis relies on a single researcher. A second reader would be a useful comparative check; the absence of an inter-coder reliability statistic follows Braun and Clarke, who do not recommend such measures for the codebook thematic analysis used here~\cite[pp.~236, 241]{braun_thematic_2022}. Severity ratings therefore remain provisional, single-evaluator judgements.

\section{Generalisability}
\label{sec:disc-generalisability}

Two questions of generalisability are separate, and are answered separately: how far the artefact would carry to another institution, and how far the findings would carry to another tool.

\paragraph{The artefact.}
The curriculum abstraction underlying the tool (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:curriculum-abstraction}) was read directly off TU~Wien's Bachelor in Computer Science and Master in Software Engineering curriculum regulations rather than designed as an institution-agnostic model. Separating what would transfer by re-parameterisation from what would need redesign gives a checkable split. Programme-agnostic are the four-level hierarchy of programme, exam subject, module and course; the plan model and the two views' division of labour; the checklist-driven loop and the Parking Stage; and the per-semester credit limits, which are the tool's own plan-sanity limits rather than curriculum law. Bound to the two TU~Wien programmes, and encoded as one rule set each (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance}), are the two obligation taxonomies, the Bachelor's StEOP course sets and its allowance of credits before the gate, its seven focus areas and their alias table, its variant-mixing rule and its transferable-skills cap, and the Master's core sets per exam subject, its subject-modules minimum, its advanced-topics prefixes and its two enforced prerequisite pairs. A third programme therefore means a third rule set, not a re-parameterisation, and how much of the rule sets could be shared is the question Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance} leaves to that point.

\paragraph{The findings.}
The thesis's separable outputs, stated as contributions in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}), generalise differently. The requirements specification and the process findings are informative for study-planning tools in general. The hybrid-specific findings, namely the graph's position in the planning cycle, its dependence on filters, and the Parking Stage's function as a hinge, are evidence for designing hybrid interfaces specifically, not evidence that a hybrid is the right choice for every planner.
