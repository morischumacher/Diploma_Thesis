\label{chap:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} reported what eleven students did and said with one tool. This chapter steps back from the sessions to the design and to the literature: it asks what the findings say about the assumptions the design was built on, what they mean for hybrid planning interfaces in general, and where they confirm, qualify or extend the work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. It then records the capabilities the study asked for, the limitations that bound the findings, and the work that follows. Throughout, it distinguishes what the study \emph{establishes} from what it \emph{suggests}. The study was scaled on purpose: eleven students observed deeply rather than many observed thinly, because a reliable estimate of the graph's effect would have needed a counterbalanced cohort several times larger, which a master's thesis cannot recruit, and a small sample studied at the level of every frame yields something the larger study would not, a description of the planning process itself. The findings are therefore of two kinds, descriptive results that the recordings establish, and hypotheses the study generates for a larger one, and each is marked. To the author's knowledge, and on the review of Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}, no prior study reports how students plan with a graph-and-table interface at the level of process; the systems closest to this one were evaluated for reception rather than for use~\cite{trippel_developing_2025}. This chapter is written as the exploratory start that position implies.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:disc-rq-answers}

Before interpreting the findings, this section states plainly what each research question was answered with, and how completely.

\paragraph{Research Question 1: students' needs and the requirements that follow.}
The answer is the specification of Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}: 13 features carrying 55 numbered requirements, each traceable through a theme to the interview excerpts that motivated it, and ranked by an importance score that governed what was built first. It is answered on the evidence of six students at one faculty, which bounds it in the way Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} states: the specification documents what this population needed, not what students in general need. Two parts of the question are answered less completely than the rest. The needs concerning course dependencies were elicited and specified (Feature~\ref{feat:003}, Req.~2 and~3), but the curricula in scope turned out to encode little sequence to act on (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:graph-ltr}): the Bachelor curriculum fixes two orderings between named courses, and the evaluated system drew those two in the graph and reported them as advisories in the compliance layer, so the requirement is met on a relation that is nearly empty. And the specification is a statement of needs rather than a validated instrument: it was not tested against an alternative elicitation, and four of its requirements were not carried into the implemented system, which the delineations of Features~\ref{feat:005} and~\ref{feat:013} record.

\paragraph{Research Question 2: how such an interface can be designed and implemented.}
The answer is the artefact and, more usefully, the reasoning recorded alongside it. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} answers the design half by deriving each decision from a requirement, a prototype assumption, published guidance, or a stated design judgement, and by naming the alternative it was weighed against where one was live; Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} answers the implementation half with a working system whose central structural decision, a single authoritative plan model from which both views and all feedback are derived, is what makes the two views projections of one plan rather than two plans to reconcile. The question asks \emph{how} such an interface can be built, and a single realisation cannot establish that this is the only way or the best way to build one; what it establishes is that it can be built, and at what cost in specific decisions.

\paragraph{Research Question 3: how students plan with the interface, and what the graph contributes.}
This is the question the thesis answers most fully. How students plan: under this tool, planning is a single checklist-driven loop, the same for every participant, in which the Dashboard's missing-requirements list is read, a candidate is found and held, placed, and the list re-read; the loop's period does not change with the graph. What the graph contributes: it substitutes for the catalogue at the query step of that loop rather than adding a station, and it does not support the commit step, for the structural reason that it shows no semester dimension; its value sat in the query step and largely in its filters. Read through the three lenses Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} names, the answer holds as follows. The process was read from the path through the interface rather than from elapsed time, because at eleven participants and with a fixed order no timing could have been attributed to the tool. The plans produced were read from an audit and not from what students reported, and the two disagree: students met the constraints the tool can represent in seventeen of twenty-one resolved runs and the full brief in six of twenty-two, while every participant reported success. Satisfaction was read from self-report and is high and consistent, with pragmatic quality above hedonic and perspicuity the weakest scale, which matches the terminology and learnability problems the observational record shows; with the author as moderator and no comparison condition it carries an upward bias, and its informative part is the profile rather than the level. One component of the interface is answered more narrowly than the rest. The Recommendation Panel was measured and its use characterised, but every family answers from what a student supplies, from static catalogue and curriculum data, or from a synthetic cohort, and the deliberately simple matching behind them was never the object of study (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-recommender}). So what was evaluated is the recommendation \emph{workflow}, not the value of the suggestions it carried.

\section{The Design's Assumptions, Revisited}
\label{sec:disc-assumptions}

The design of Chapter~\ref{chap:design} rested on assumptions it could not test, some inherited from the formative study and some made as design judgements. The evaluation is the first place each meets students working with the built tool, and this section states, for each, what the findings now say about it.

\paragraph{Graph for exploration, table for commitment.}
The division of labour between the views was carried over from the prototype and the formative study, where participants said the \enquote{graph [is] used for exploration, not planning} (Section~\ref{sec:mutual-exclusion}), and the design made it a rule by giving the centre panel to one view at a time. The evaluation confirms the division and sharpens it. Confirmed, because the graph served the query step and the table the commit step for every participant who used both, and the reason is structural rather than a preference: only the table shows the semester dimension the commit step acts against (Section~\ref{sec:eval-graphrole}). Sharpened, because the graph did not merely sit beside the table as an exploration surface; it took over the exact station in the planning loop that the catalogue had held, at the catalogue's occupancy and entry count (Section~\ref{sec:eval-substitution}). \enquote{Exploration} in the formative study meant open-ended browsing; what students did in the graph was directed querying, turning a named gap into a candidate set. The assumption held, but the activity it named was narrower than the word suggested.

\paragraph{A staging area between finding and committing.}
The three-stage workflow of Section~\ref{sec:workflow}, explore, shortlist, assign, assumed that students would want to hold a course before placing it. Every participant did (E-G06, 11/11), and the Parking Stage turned out to be the mechanism by which work found in one view reached the other, which is more than the design claimed for it. The cost the design did not anticipate is that a holding area outside the plan's accounting is invisible to every completion readout (E-P38, 10/11).

\paragraph{The Dashboard as the student's compass.}
The Dashboard was designed to answer \enquote{where am I right now?} at four levels of aggregation (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard}). It did more than that: its missing-requirements list became the driver of the entire planning process, the station to which every excursion returned (E-G15, 11/11). The assumption held beyond its intention, and that is exactly why its boundary mattered. Because participants met the curriculum only through the Dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}), whatever it did not model did not exist for them, and the two constraints it omitted were the two that plans failed on (Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}).

\paragraph{Guide rather than block.}
The compliance design distinguished hard violations that block from soft advisories that inform, so that the tool would guide rather than obstruct (Section~\ref{sec:compliance}; Section~\ref{sec:overall-scope}). Students noticed and used the feedback (E-G04, 11/11), so the principle worked as intended for the rules it covered. Two of its consequences were not intended. The asymmetry between a blocking ceiling and an advisory floor let under-filled semesters pass unremarked in every run where one occurred (E-P35, 10/11), and thresholds that the student sets rather than the programme meant that the checker enforced the task only for the students who had configured it (Section~\ref{sec:eval-config}). Guidance was the right register; what was under-specified was which constraints are guidance and which are the task.

\paragraph{Recommendations with reasons, beside the plan.}
The Recommendation Panel was designed as an independently toggleable drawer whose cards carry a human-readable reason (Section~\ref{sec:rec-panel}). The reasons were read and the panel was used by ten of eleven, but it never became part of the loop: students who could judge the curriculum for themselves browsed it and returned to the checklist (E-N05, 7/11), and the reasons identified a relationship without saying which of the student's own inputs had produced it (E-P68, 4/9). The assumption that a reasoned suggestion is welcome held; the assumption that it would drive the process did not, except for the two participants with the least curriculum knowledge, which is the exploratory pattern stated below.

\paragraph{An overlay that draws only what the curriculum fixes.}
The prerequisite overlay was scoped to the pairs the curriculum itself states, so that an edge would carry one meaning (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-prereq-edges}). The cost of that scope was visible: with two such pairs in the Bachelor curriculum the overlay was nearly empty, four participants asked for ordering information at the point of placement (E-P44, 4/11), and what the graph made legible was containment rather than sequence. The design assumption was sound as a rule of legibility; what it met was a curriculum with almost nothing to be legible about, which is the point Section~\ref{sec:disc-related} takes up.

\paragraph{The two upstream assumptions.}
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} named two assumptions the whole thesis rests on (Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions}). On the hybrid concept, the evaluation says only that the two surfaces served different steps of one loop and that seven of eleven participants articulated that division as the value of having both (E-N01, 7/11); no non-hybrid alternative was run, so whether a single surface carrying the graph's filters could have served both steps is the first hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}. On what a curriculum encodes, the finding is not the evaluation's but the curriculum's: the regulations in scope publish almost no sequencing, and that was readable at design time. The evaluation adds only that students used the graph for what such a curriculum does afford, orientation and filtered browsing.

\section{Interpretation of Findings}
\label{sec:disc-interpretation}

\paragraph{The primary result is a process, not a feature.}
The clearest answer to RQ3 is that students converged on one stable way of working, and that this loop, rather than any single component, is what made the tool usable. The pre-fill seeds a plan, the requirement checklist drives it, the recommendations propose candidates, the Parking Stage stages them, and the table commits them. Three properties make this more than a description of the interface. First, the loop is the same for the fluent, unassisted participants and for the most junior participant who needed to be shown where it started, so it is a property of the artefact rather than of facilitation. Second, it is measurable: its two-station cycle accounts for roughly 40~\% of all observed state changes in both conditions, and its period is unchanged by the availability of the graph. Third, the loop externalises the parts of programme planning that students cannot hold in their heads, namely which requirements remain open and what would close them. This reframes what it means to say the tool works: it works because the guidance surfaces carry the bookkeeping, allowing students to spend their attention on choosing rather than on tracking.

\paragraph{The tool was the curriculum.}
Participants met the curriculum's rules only through the Dashboard, since no brief stated them (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}). The tool therefore did not support a student's understanding of the curriculum; for the duration of a task, it constituted it. This is the general form of several specific findings: terminology the tool did not define stayed unclear (E-P01), a requirement the tool did not model was optimised away, and the two least confident participants used the Dashboard and the recommendations as a substitute for curriculum knowledge (E-N06). A planning tool that is the student's only channel to the rules inherits the obligation to be complete, which is a stronger requirement than being correct.

\paragraph{The Parking Stage is the mechanism that makes a two-view plan coherent.}
A hybrid interface over a shared plan raises an obvious question: how does work discovered in one view reach the other without either view having to duplicate the other's function? The evaluation answers it concretely. The Parking Stage is a staging buffer that sits outside the plan's accounting, and it is where courses found in the graph, or surfaced by a recommendation, wait until they are assigned in the table. Every participant used it this way. We regard this as the study's most transferable structural finding: a shared-plan hybrid does not need each view to be able to do everything, but it does need an explicit place for a decision that has been made but not yet committed. The same property has a cost, in that parked credits are invisible to every completion readout, which several participants had to compensate for mentally.

\paragraph{The graph occupies a place in the process rather than adding one.}
The most precise result of the evaluation is positional. When the graph becomes available it does not extend the planning cycle with a third station; it takes over the station the catalogue previously held. Catalogue occupancy falls from 27.1~\% to 6.7~\% of sampled frames while the graph accounts for 26.6~\%, the catalogue is entered forty times across the graph-off runs against forty-two graph entries across the graph-on runs, and the probability of moving from the working state to the catalogue falls to zero. The cycle keeps its shape and its period. What changes is the surface on which a named requirement is turned into candidate courses.

This is a stronger statement than the observation that participants liked the graph, and it is more useful for design. It says that the contribution of a hybrid structural view, in this tool and this curriculum, is to the \emph{query} step of planning and not to the \emph{commit} step.
Read through cognitive fit~\cite{vessey1991cognitive}, a representation aids performance when it matches the task, and the substitution locates the seam precisely: allocation across semesters is a sequential-scheduling task the table's lanes fit, whereas turning an open requirement into a candidate set is a filtering and orientation task that a browsable hierarchy fits better than a scrollable list.

\paragraph{Where the graph's value sat: the query step, and largely its filters.}
The substitution says where the graph entered; the participants who read it most fluently said what they entered it for. They judged the node--link view close to redundant and attributed what they gained in the graph-on condition to the filter sidebar, which exists only beside the graph and which the table and catalogue lack (E-G07, 11/11; E-P04, 11/11). The recordings show the same thing without the judgement: participants entered a view several of them found hard to read in order to reach a control they found essential, and one switched into the graph for the single purpose of finding a course of a given ECTS value, a query the catalogue cannot express. For the design of hybrid planners this is the most consequential finding the study produced, because it locates the value of the graph-on condition in a component that is separable from the visualisation. It does not say the graph is worthless: interest-driven browsing (E-G10, 9/11) and module composition (E-G17, 6/11) are things the filters do not give. It says that the study cannot tell how much of the graph's contribution would survive if the table had the filters, and Section~\ref{sec:disc-future} states that as the hypothesis it is.

\paragraph{What the graph did not change is as informative as what it did.}
The share of placement attempts ending in a rejection does not move consistently between conditions: it falls for seven participants and rises for four, a split consistent with chance ($p = 0.55$). The trial-and-error loop at the commit stage therefore survives the introduction of the graph untouched. This is coherent with the positional finding rather than in tension with it. The rework arises because term parity and per-lane load are not shown on the semester lane at the moment of placement, which is a property of the table; a surface that improves how candidates are found cannot repair how they are committed.

\paragraph{Placement returns to the table because of what the graph does not display.}
Substantive plan changes were made inside the graph by participants across the experience range, so the graph is not a read-only inspector. The asymmetry that holds is narrower: semester lanes and their ECTS totals are not rendered in Graph View at any zoom level, so a student placing a course from a node cannot see the load consequence of the placement. One participant breached a scenario's reduced-load cap this way and discovered it only after switching back, and two named the missing information as their reason for not placing from the graph at all. The division of labour between the two views is therefore not primarily a matter of preference or of representational fit; it follows from the fact that only one of the views shows the constraint being acted against. Stated generally, a second view can be committed from only if it carries enough of the plan's state to show the consequence of the commitment.

\paragraph{A user-supplied compliance layer checks the wrong thing for the users who do not supply it.}
Most participants adapted the profile's workload thresholds to their scenario's stated band before or during planning, and the engine then enforced what they had set. Those who did not were checked against the tool's defaults instead, and in one case a scenario was completed with a semester well outside the stated band while the Dashboard reported no warning. The setting is discoverable and it works; the design consequence is that a rule-checker parameterised by the user silently mis-checks for exactly the students least likely to parameterise it, who are also the students least able to notice. The complementary asymmetry compounds it: across all twenty-two runs the upper bound blocks with a rejection while the lower bound only advises, so an under-filled semester is never forced to correct.

\paragraph{Explainable recommendations are scaffolding whose value scales inversely with domain confidence (exploratory).}
The Recommendation Panel was used differently in a patterned way: participants who could not yet judge the curriculum leaned on it as a substitute for domain knowledge, while those who knew the curriculum browsed and then declined it in favour of the explicit checklist and catalogue search, despite the engine visibly responding to their stated interests. This suggests the explainable recommendation is most valuable to the least-expert user and that confident users want a different route to the same end. Given the sample, we offer this as an exploratory pattern rather than an established effect, but its design consequence is clear and is stated below.

\paragraph{Self-report measured felt completion, and the checklist's boundary is where plans failed.}
Every one of the eleven participants rated their Scenario~A performance a success, and no Scenario~A run met the stated constraints. The tool's own completion signal ran ahead of the observed state in the same way, reporting no missing requirements at the end of all twenty-two runs. This is a methodological reason to treat the recording, not the questionnaire, as the measure of what was produced, rather than a headline failure of the tool. The instructive case is sharper than a general say-do gap. One participant created an empty semester lane specifically to serve as the mandated internship semester, and then filled it because the checklist reported missing ECTS and knew nothing about internships. A constraint the student had represented himself was invisible to the mechanism he was following, so following the mechanism removed it. The lesson is not that students over-report; it is that a checklist students trust as their compass must cover every constraint they are held to, because whatever it omits is what gets optimised away.

\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:disc-implications}

Each implication below is a general rule for a planning interface of this kind, followed by the observation in this study that produced it.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Put the filters in the surface where work happens.} Placement occurs in the table, and the filter set exists only beside the graph. The substitution result gives this implication its force: if the graph is used as the station at which requirements become candidates, and if what participants valued there is separable from the visualisation, then bringing the same filters into the table and catalogue should deliver much of what the graph-on condition delivered. A testable prediction follows in Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}.
    \item \textbf{Show the constraint in the view where it can be violated.} A view that permits placement must display the load and term consequences of that placement. Rendering semester totals, or at minimum the target lane's state, inside the graph would remove the study's clearest instance of an invisible constraint breach.
    \item \textbf{Repair the commit step in the surface that owns it.} The placement rework loop was untouched by the graph and remains untouched by any addition to the finding step. Term parity on the lane and valid targets marked before the drop address it directly, using information the tool already computes.
    \item \textbf{Make the completion signal cover the whole task.} Every hard constraint a student is held to should be a first-class, tracked requirement in the checklist, including the reduced-load semester and the per-semester band, which were not modelled. This matters most for the students least able to catch an omission, and it converts the study's most effective surface into a trustworthy signal rather than a partial one.
    \item \textbf{Count what is staged.} A holding area is necessary for a two-view plan, but a total that silently excludes it is a wrong total. Parked credits should be visible in every readout that a student reads as completion, either counted separately or flagged as uncounted (E-P38, 10/11).
    \item \textbf{Make status glanceable.} Requirement status behind collapsible panels produced repeated expand-and-collapse round trips, and in four sessions the warnings panel was never opened at all while its counter climbed to five. A warning nobody opens is not a warning.
    \item \textbf{Give a rule-checker safe defaults, not just configurable ones.} Where thresholds are user-supplied, the tool should either derive them from the programme or state plainly which value it is checking against, so that an unconfigured checker does not silently validate against the wrong band, and it should treat a stated range as a range rather than as a single ceiling.
    \item \textbf{Serve both the recommendation route and the checklist-plus-filter route.} Reasoned recommendations help the student without a curriculum model; the confident student reaches for the explicit checklist and a filterable catalogue. Both should be first-class.
\end{itemize}

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings both confirm and qualify the work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. They \emph{confirm}, in a different tool and sample, two of the problems Bartel et al.~\cite{bartel_design_2024} report for the study-planning assistant they evaluated, namely difficulty with curriculum terminology and the hiddenness of extended search and filter features. That the tool was designed under those principles and still exhibits both is a useful, candid result: the principles name the right hazards without guaranteeing immunity to them.

They \emph{qualify} the graph-visualisation strand in a way that bears on its premises. That literature, from concept mapping and \emph{ViCurriAS}~\cite{zucker_vicurrias_2009} through \emph{STOPS}~\cite{auvinen_stops_2014} to the elective-focused graph of Trippel and Röpke~\cite{trippel_developing_2025}, is built on the assumption that a curriculum carries a prerequisite structure worth visualising; \emph{STOPS} decomposes courses into prerequisite-linked outcomes, and Trippel and Röpke distinguish required from recommended edges. The curriculum in this study largely does not carry that structure. Outside the introductory phase and ordering within a module, the regulations specify little sequencing, which is why the implemented graph is dominated by containment and carries a prerequisite overlay of two edges, and why a prerequisite advisory arose only once in twenty-two runs of planning. The observation generalises beyond this artefact: where a curriculum encodes few hard dependencies, a dependency-oriented visualisation has little to show, and the value of a curriculum graph shifts from revealing sequence to supporting orientation and filtered browsing. That is what students used it for here, and the substitution result of Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation} says so quantitatively rather than impressionistically.

They \emph{extend} the AIStudyBuddy line~\cite{roepke_study_2024, judel_supporting_2023, trippel_developing_2025} in two ways. First, the closely related result that a graph-based curriculum visualisation is well received~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} is reproduced here at the level of affect but qualified at the level of use: the same graph functioned as a query surface that displaced the catalogue rather than as a planning surface, and the participants who read it most fluently judged it substitutable by its own filters. Second, where that line separates a student application from institution-facing analytics~\cite{roepke_study_2024}, this study contributes a student-side, in-workflow account that institutional analytics cannot surface, namely how the completion signal and the recommendations are experienced while a plan is being formed, including the felt-versus-actual completion gap and the boundary effect described in Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

The evaluation took one direction through a tool that admits many, and its limitations are those of that direction rather than of the artefact. Most of them are the known price of a decision made on purpose, to study eleven students at the level of every frame rather than a cohort at the level of a questionnaire, and each is stated with what it costs. They bound what the findings can be read to mean; the directions not taken are the subject of Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No causal claim about the graph.} The two scenarios differ simultaneously in the graph, the persona content, and presentation order, and the order was fixed by design (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}). Of the process measures reported, only the displacement of the catalogue is attributable to graph availability; the change in placement rate and the concentration of compliant runs in the second scenario are reported as descriptions of the second run.
    \item \textbf{The design partly guarantees the shape of the graph finding.} Because the graph was available only after a workflow had formed without it, the observation that it enters as an excursion into an established loop is in part a consequence of the design rather than a discovery about the graph. The claim is therefore scoped rather than defended: \emph{where a structural view is introduced into an already-practised planning workflow, it is used for querying rather than committing, and it displaces the surface that previously served the query step}. That remains a real finding, and it is arguably the realistic case, since students commonly learn a tool and then discover its further features rather than meeting everything at once. What the design cannot address is how a student who met the graph first would plan, which Section~\ref{sec:disc-future} treats as a separate condition rather than as the same one reversed.
    \item \textbf{The moderator was the designer.} The sessions were moderated by the author, who designed and built the tool and coded the sessions afterwards. Participants knew this, and self-report under those conditions is exposed to demand characteristics, which is one reason the questionnaire means sit near the ceiling of both instruments and are read as the weakest evidence in the chapter. A master's thesis cannot separate the roles; the observational record is the compensation, since what a participant did on screen is not a courtesy to the moderator.
    \item \textbf{The two scenarios differ in more than the graph.} The personas differ in focus area, the briefs in the number of semesters and in the reduced-load position, so the second run is a bundle of order and task difference. The substitution argument survives the bundle, because neither practice nor a change of topic explains abandoning a working catalogue for a filter panel that did not exist in the first run; the second-run observations do not, and are reported as such.
    \item \textbf{Facilitator assistance in every session.} A moderator resolved operational and conceptual difficulties in every session, and in several cases unblocked task-blocking issues; four scenario runs were ended by the moderator rather than by the participant. Task outcomes are therefore facilitator-qualified rather than independent. For the four most recent participants the extent of that assistance cannot be quantified at all, because their recordings are screen shares with the webcam tile cropped and their transcripts carry a single speaker label, so moderator and participant speech are not separable.
    \item \textbf{Two recording cohorts.} Seven sessions were captured as direct screen recordings of 15 to 30 minutes per scenario; four were captured as screen shares within a video call, of 6 to 12 minutes per scenario and at lower effective resolution. Shorter sessions give a problem less opportunity to appear, so a frequency that fell as a proportion across the full cohort may reflect session length rather than the interface. Lane values in the second cohort are reconciled arithmetically against the Dashboard total rather than read from the lane header, and are reported as unknown where that does not determine them.
    \item \textbf{Small, computing-heavy sample.} Eleven analysed sessions from computing programmes at a single institution. This bounds generalisation and bears specifically on the reading of the graph: computing students are unusually fluent with node--link representations, so a general student population would if anything find the graph harder rather than easier.
    \item \textbf{Two records, but only two.} The analysis rests on the screen recording and the think-aloud transcript. Neither captures intent that was neither spoken nor visible, and the open-exploration and debrief phases were not covered by the frame sampling in every session, so several codes rest on transcript evidence alone.
    \item \textbf{Single-researcher analysis.} Coding, adjudication between the two records, and severity rating were performed by one researcher. The absent check is a second reader to compare interpretations with, not a reliability statistic: Braun and Clarke do not recommend inter-coder reliability measures for the codebook thematic analysis used here, and treat them as a criterion belonging to a different branch~\cite[pp.~236, 241]{braun_thematic_2022}. Severities remain provisional single-evaluator judgements. Applying the final codebook to all participants removes the ordering artefact in the frequencies but does not substitute for a second reader.
    \item \textbf{Instrumentation.} The application's per-scenario timer froze while its browser tab was backgrounded and is not used; in three sessions the recording ended or froze while work continued, so a final observed frame can understate what was achieved. Ten-second sampling also means transient banners shorter than the interval may be missed, so counts of such events are lower bounds.
    \item \textbf{The acceptance instrument is TAM-derived, not the validated TAM.} Three items per construct rather than six, reworded to the planning domain and answered on an agreement rather than a likelihood scale; one ease-of-use item measures a task outcome rather than a cost of operation and is reported separately for that reason (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-questionnaire}). No reliability claim is made for the shortened scales.
    \item \textbf{Exploratory patterns.} The expertise-moderated use of recommendations, and the observation that the graph appeared to help some participants and hinder others, rest on few and in places single-participant contrasts with unmeasured, confounded moderators. They are framed as hypotheses for a powered study.
\end{itemize}

\section{Generalisability}
\label{sec:disc-generalisability}

A generalisability question specific to the artefact concerns how much of the design is tied to its evaluation context. The curriculum abstraction underlying the tool (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:curriculum-abstraction}) was read directly off TU~Wien's Bachelor Informatics and Master Software Engineering curriculum regulations rather than designed as an institution-agnostic model. Separating what would transfer by re-parameterisation from what would need redesign gives a checkable split. Programme-agnostic are the four-level hierarchy of programme, exam subject, module and course; the plan model and the two views' division of labour; the checklist-driven loop and the Parking Stage; and the per-semester credit limits, which are the tool's own plan-sanity limits rather than curriculum law. Bound to the two TU~Wien programmes, and encoded as one rule set each (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance}), are the two obligation taxonomies, the Bachelor's StEOP course sets and its allowance of credits before the gate, its seven focus areas and their alias table, its variant-mixing rule and its transferable-skills cap, and the Master's core sets per exam subject, its subject-modules minimum, its advanced-topics prefixes and its two enforced prerequisite pairs. A third programme therefore means a third rule set, not a re-parameterisation, and how much of the rule sets could be shared is the question Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance} leaves to that point.

More broadly, the thesis's separable outputs, stated as contributions in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}), generalise differently. The requirements specification and the process findings are informative for study-planning tools in general. The hybrid-specific findings, namely the graph's position in the planning cycle, its dependence on filters, and the Parking Stage's function as a hinge, are evidence for designing hybrid interfaces specifically, not evidence that a hybrid is the right choice for every planner.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:disc-future}

The implemented system is a platform on which a programme of evaluation can be built, and the study reported here is its first direction, not its only one. The same tool supports evaluation along axes this thesis did not pursue: the quality and acceptance of the recommendation channels considered on their own, the effect of the compliance layer on plan correctness under controlled prior knowledge, longitudinal use across a real enrolment period rather than a laboratory session, comparison against the institutional tools students currently use, and larger and broader cohorts than eleven computing students. Within the direction this thesis did take, five questions follow directly from the findings.

First, the study yields a falsifiable design hypothesis it could not itself test. If the value participants attributed to the graph-on condition is carried by the filters rather than by the node--link view, then bringing the full filter set into the table should render a separate graph mode largely optional. The substitution result sharpens the prediction: with filters in the table, the query station should stay in the table rather than migrating to the graph, and the graph's occupancy should fall towards the catalogue's level in the graph-on condition. This is also the test the hybrid concept itself still owes.

Second, a graph-first condition is a different experiment rather than this one reversed. With the graph present during initial learning, it may be folded into the base loop rather than remaining an excursion, or, on the evidence of the most junior participant, it may overload a student who has no simpler fallback workflow. Both are testable in a powered study and neither is answerable at this sample size.

Third, the capabilities of Section~\ref{sec:disc-unmet} are a development agenda, of which the reduced-load semester, the enforced workload band, the per-lane term indicator and per-lane load inside the graph most visibly shaped the observed process; the term indicator is the one change predicted to move the placement-rework measure the graph left unchanged, lane totals in the graph are the one change that would let commit support be tested rather than found absent, and implementing them makes the completion signal a test object in its own right.

Fourth, the process account should be repeated as a log study. The plan model already stamps every change (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-frontend}), so an event log written by the tool would give exact entries, dwell, placements and rejections for every session with no sampling and no labelling, and it would scale to the cohort this study could not recruit: a few hundred students planning their own programmes over a semester rather than eleven planning a persona's in an hour. That is the study that could turn the loop, the substitution and the calibration gap from descriptions into estimates, and it would answer with data what this thesis can only pose as its first hypothesis, whether the graph earns its place once the table has its filters.

Fifth, prerequisite visibility now has an artefact to test rather than a proposal to weigh. The soft-dependency edge layer described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} (Section~\ref{sec:impl-prereq-edges}) was built after the study and draws the curricula's expected-knowledge notes beside the pairs the evaluated graph already drew. Because the pairs themselves are a handful in each programme, the question it raises is not whether students can read prerequisite edges but whether so few of them change how a plan is built, and whether a view of the eligibility gates these curricula do encode, StEOP and core-before-elective, would serve the same need better than a view of sequence.

Beyond these, the patterns offered here as exploratory, in particular the expertise-moderated use of recommendations, warrant a powered study with an explicit measure of the relevant individual difference and a sample drawn beyond computing programmes.
