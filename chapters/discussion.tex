\label{chap:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} reported participant behavior and feedback regarding the evaluated artefact. This chapter abstracts from these individual sessions to evaluate the underlying design principles and connect the findings to existing literature. Specifically, it examines the validity of the initial design assumptions, articulates the implications for hybrid planning interfaces, and situates the results within the prior work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. It subsequently catalogues the requested system capabilities and delineates the methodological limitations bounding the findings. The formal answers to the research questions and directions for future work are reserved for Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}. 

Throughout, this chapter explicitly distinguishes between what the study \emph{establishes} (descriptive results empirically grounded in the recordings) and what it \emph{suggests} (exploratory hypotheses generated for future validation). To the author's knowledge, no prior literature reports how students plan with a hybrid graph-and-table interface at the process level; the systems closest to this artefact were evaluated solely for user reception rather than operational use~\cite{trippel_developing_2025}. Because this study maps new behavioral territory, the chapter is structured as an exploratory foundation.

\section{The Design's Assumptions, Revisited}
\label{sec:disc-assumptions}

The system design (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}) rested on untested assumptions derived from the formative study and heuristic judgements. The evaluation phase provided the empirical basis to test these premises against active user behavior. This section systematically reviews each assumption in light of the observational findings.

\paragraph{Graph for exploration, table for commitment.}
The functional division between the views originated from the formative study, where participants indicated the \enquote{graph [is] used for exploration, not planning} (Section~\ref{sec:mutual-exclusion}). The interface formalized this division via mutually exclusive centre panels. The evaluation empirically validates and refines this division. It validates it structurally: the graph facilitated the query phase while the table served the commit phase for all participants who utilized both, dictated by the table's exclusive rendering of the temporal semester dimension (Section~\ref{sec:eval-graphrole}). It refines it behaviorally: the graph functioned not merely as an unstructured exploratory surface, but systematically displaced the course catalogue within the planning loop, matching its historical occupancy and entry metrics (Section~\ref{sec:eval-substitution}). While \enquote{exploration} initially implied open-ended browsing, participant behavior demonstrated directed querying to translate a specific requirement gap into a candidate set. Thus, the assumption holds, though the observed behavior is significantly more structured than initially hypothesized.

\paragraph{A staging area between finding and committing.}
The three-stage workflow (explore, shortlist, assign; Section~\ref{sec:workflow}) assumed users require a buffer to hold courses prior to final placement. The data strongly supports this (E-G06, 11/11); moreover, the Parking Stage functioned as the critical data bridge allowing candidate courses to persist across view switches, exceeding its original design scope. However, an unanticipated architectural cost emerged: because the staging area operates outside the active plan's accounting, parked credits remained invisible to system completion metrics (E-P38, 10/11).

\paragraph{The Dashboard as the student's compass.}
The Dashboard was designed to provide orientation across four levels of aggregation (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard}). In practice, it assumed a more determinative role: its missing-requirements list functioned as the primary engine for the entire planning process, serving as the baseline state for every task excursion (E-G15, 11/11). Because participants interacted with the curriculum exclusively through this Dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}), constraints omitted from its model effectively ceased to exist in the users' mental models. Consequently, the two constraints the Dashboard failed to track were precisely the constraints that caused the plans to fail (Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}).

\paragraph{Guide rather than block.}
The compliance engine differentiated between hard blocking violations and soft advisories to guide rather than obstruct user actions (Section~\ref{sec:compliance}; Section~\ref{sec:overall-scope}). Participants consistently noticed and integrated this feedback (E-G04, 11/11), validating the principle for the explicitly modeled rules. However, two unintended systemic consequences emerged. First, the asymmetry between a blocking ceiling and an advisory floor allowed under-filled semesters to pass without correction in all applicable runs (E-P35, 10/11). Second, relying on user-configured, rather than programme-defined, thresholds meant the system only enforced the task brief for participants who manually adjusted their profiles (Section~\ref{sec:eval-config}). While the guidance paradigm proved effective, the rigid delineation between advisory feedback and hard constraints requires more robust systemic specification.

\paragraph{Recommendations with reasons, beside the plan.}
The Recommendation Panel was implemented as an independent UI overlay featuring cards with human-readable rationales (Section~\ref{sec:rec-panel}). Although ten participants utilized the panel and read the rationales, it failed to integrate into the primary planning loop. Participants possessing sufficient curriculum knowledge browsed the suggestions but invariably reverted to the deterministic checklist and catalogue search (E-N05, 7/11). Furthermore, while the rationales identified a module relationship, they failed to specify which user input triggered the suggestion, reducing systemic transparency (E-P68, 4/9). The assumption that users welcome explainable suggestions is supported; however, the hypothesis that recommendations would actively drive the workflow is rejected, save for the participants exhibiting the lowest domain knowledge (detailed as an exploratory pattern below).

\paragraph{An overlay that draws only what the curriculum fixes.}
The prerequisite overlay was strictly scoped to relations explicitly codified by the curriculum, ensuring edges carried unambiguous semantics (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-prereq-edges}). The operational cost of this strict scoping was substantial: because the Bachelor curriculum codifies only two such pairs, the overlay remained largely unpopulated, prompting four participants to request just-in-time ordering information at the point of placement (E-P44, 4/11). While the assumption holds as a principle of visual legibility, it is mismatched with a curriculum lacking dense formal sequencing dependencies, a dynamic further analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:disc-related}.

\paragraph{The two upstream assumptions.}
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} established two foundational premises for the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions}). Regarding the hybrid interface concept, the evaluation confirms that the two surfaces successfully supported distinct phases of a single planning loop, with seven participants explicitly citing this functional division as the primary benefit of the dual-view system (E-N01, 7/11). Because the study did not evaluate a non-hybrid baseline, the question of whether a unified surface equipped with equivalent filters could achieve similar outcomes remains an open hypothesis for Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}). Regarding the structural density of curricula, the findings reflect the domain rather than the tool: the targeted regulations publish minimal sequencing requirements. Consequently, the evaluation demonstrates that in the absence of dense dependency networks, users leverage curriculum graphs primarily for structural orientation and filtered browsing rather than sequential pathfinding.

\section{Interpretation of Findings}
\label{sec:disc-interpretation}

\paragraph{The primary result is a process, not a feature.}
The primary answer to RQ3 is that participants converged on a stable workflow loop. This procedural structure, rather than any isolated feature, drove system usability. The workflow consists of seeding the plan via pre-fill, driving completion via the checklist, proposing candidates via recommendations, catalog (and graph), staging via the Parking Stage, and committing via the table. Three properties elevate this finding. First, this loop emerged identically for independent experts and assisted novices, indicating it is an architectural property of the artefact rather than an artifact of moderator facilitation. Second, it is structurally stable: its two-station cycle accounts for roughly 40~\% of all observed state changes, and its temporal period is unaffected by graph availability. Third, the loop externalizes working memory. The system succeeds by offloading cognitive bookkeeping to the guidance surfaces, allowing users to allocate attentional resources to decision-making rather than constraint tracking.

\paragraph{The tool was the curriculum.}
Because the task briefs omitted structural constraints, participants interacted with curriculum rules exclusively via the Dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}). Consequently, the tool did not merely support curriculum comprehension; it effectively constituted the curriculum. This dynamic underpins several observed friction points: terminology undefined by the tool remained illegible (E-P01), unmodeled constraints were optimized away, and novices utilized the Dashboard as a direct substitute for domain knowledge (E-N06). When an interface serves as the exclusive channel for domain rules, it assumes an obligation for systemic completeness, which supersedes mere computational correctness.

\paragraph{The Parking Stage is the mechanism that makes a two-view plan coherent.}
A shared-plan hybrid interface necessitates a mechanism to transfer state between views without duplicating functionality. The evaluation establishes the Parking Stage as this mechanism: it operates as an intermediate buffer for courses transitioning from graph-based discovery to tabular commitment. This establishes a highly transferable architectural finding: hybrid views do not require symmetric capabilities, provided an explicit buffer exists for uncommitted decisions. However, this architecture introduces a specific cognitive cost: because parked credits sit outside the active plan's accounting, they remain invisible to completion metrics, requiring manual user compensation.

\paragraph{The graph occupies a place in the process rather than adding one.}
The evaluation yields a structural finding: the graph does not introduce a third workflow station; it strictly displaces the catalogue. When graph availability toggled on, catalogue occupancy collapsed from 27.1~\% to 6.7~\%, while the graph absorbed an equivalent 26.6~\% share. The probability of navigating from the Dashboard to the catalogue fell to zero, yet the workflow cycle maintained its underlying shape and temporal period. Analyzed through Vessey's Cognitive Fit Theory~\cite{vessey1991cognitive}, which posits that performance improves when spatial representation matches task requirements, this displacement is theoretically consistent: semester allocation is a sequential-scheduling task suited to tabular lanes, whereas querying requirements is a filtering task optimized by a browsable hierarchy.

\paragraph{Where the graph's value sat: the query step, and largely its filters.}
Fluent participants explicitly attributed the graph's utility to its filter sidebar rather than the node-link visualization (E-G07, 11/11; E-P04, 11/11). The visual records corroborate this self-report. Participants utilized the graph primarily to execute precise queries, such as isolating specific ECTS values, which the tabular catalogue could not support. This indicates that the core driver of the query phase is the filter panel itself, a component conceptually separable from the spatial visualization. While the node-link graph remains effective for interest-driven browsing (E-G10) and displaying module composition (E-G17), this finding generates a consequential design hypothesis. Specifically, much of the graph's operational contribution to the planning loop might be replicated simply by porting its filters to the table view (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}).

\paragraph{Placement returns to the table because of what the graph does not display.}
Although participants executed substantive plan changes within the graph, demonstrating it is not a read-only inspector, the commit phase invariably reverted to the table. Because the Graph View abstracts away semester lanes and ECTS totals, users cannot evaluate the temporal or load consequences of direct graph placements, leading directly to constraint breaches in the observed sessions. As a general HCI principle, a secondary view can only support commitment actions if it renders sufficient state data to preview the consequences of that commitment.

\paragraph{A user-supplied compliance layer checks the wrong thing for the users who do not supply it.}
While most participants successfully configured the profile's workload thresholds to match the task brief, those who omitted this step were silently evaluated against default parameters. This resulted in objectively failed plans being validated as complete by the Dashboard. This creates a systemic vulnerability: a user-parameterized rule engine silently generates false positives for the exact cohort least likely to execute the configuration, and least equipped to detect the resulting error. Furthermore, the engine's programmatic asymmetry—where upper bounds block but lower bounds merely advise—ensured under-filled semesters systematically bypassed correction.

\paragraph{Explainable recommendations are scaffolding whose value scales inversely with domain confidence (exploratory).}
Participants lacking domain expertise utilized recommendations compensatorily, whereas knowledgeable users browsed suggestions but ultimately deferred to deterministic checklist searches, despite the engine accurately reflecting their stated interests. This indicates that explainable recommendations function primarily as cognitive scaffolding for novices rather than efficiency accelerators for experts. Given the sample size, this is presented as an exploratory behavioral pattern with direct design implications.

\paragraph{Self-report measured felt completion, and the checklist's boundary is where plans failed.}
Participants consistently rated failed plans as successful, and all 22 evaluation runs ended with the system erroneously reporting zero missing requirements (Section~\ref{app:eval-completion}). Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness} identifies the behavioral mechanism driving this discrepancy: when a user's correct mental model conflicts with an incomplete system checklist, the interface invariably supersedes human working memory. Because the system continuously broadcasts its authoritative completion state, constraints omitted from its tracking logic are not merely left unverified; they are systematically optimized away by users attempting to satisfy the interface. The fundamental design implication is that an authoritative completion checklist must rigorously encompass every external constraint; partial validation actively sabotages unmodeled requirements.

\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:disc-implications}

Each implication below is a general rule for a planning interface of this kind, followed by the observation in this study that produced it.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Put the filters in the surface where work happens.} Placement occurs in the table, and the filter set exists only beside the graph. The substitution result gives this implication its force: if the graph is used as the station at which requirements become candidates, and if what participants valued there is separable from the visualisation, then bringing the same filters into the table and catalogue should deliver much of what the graph-on condition delivered. A testable prediction follows in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}).
    \item \textbf{Show the constraint in the view where it can be violated.} A view that permits placement must display the load and term consequences of that placement. Rendering semester totals, or at minimum the target lane's state, inside the graph would remove the study's clearest instance of an invisible constraint breach.
    \item \textbf{Repair the commit step in the surface that owns it.} The placement rework loop was untouched by the graph and remains untouched by any addition to the finding step. Term parity on the lane and valid targets marked before the drop address it directly, using information the tool already computes.
    \item \textbf{Make the completion signal cover the whole task.} Every hard constraint a student is held to should be a first-class, tracked requirement in the checklist, including the reduced-load semester and the per-semester band, which were not modelled. This matters most for the students least able to catch an omission, and it converts the study's most effective surface into a trustworthy signal rather than a partial one.
    \item \textbf{Count what is staged.} A holding area is necessary for a two-view plan, but a total that silently excludes it is a wrong total. Parked credits should be visible in every readout that a student reads as completion, either counted separately or flagged as uncounted (E-P38, 10/11).
    \item \textbf{Make status glanceable.} Requirement status behind collapsible panels produced repeated expand-and-collapse round trips, and in four sessions the warnings panel was never opened at all while its counter climbed to five. A warning nobody opens is not a warning.
    \item \textbf{Give a rule-checker safe defaults, not just configurable ones.} Where thresholds are user-supplied, the tool should either derive them from the programme or state plainly which value it is checking against, so that an unconfigured checker does not silently validate against the wrong band, and it should treat a stated range as a range rather than as a single ceiling.
    \item \textbf{Serve both the recommendation route and the checklist-plus-filter route.} Reasoned recommendations help the student without a curriculum model; the confident student reaches for the explicit checklist and a filterable catalogue. Both should be first-class.
\end{itemize}

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings both confirm and qualify the work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. They \emph{confirm}, in a different tool and sample, two of the problems Bartel et al.~\cite{bartel_design_2024} report for the study-planning assistant they evaluated, namely difficulty with curriculum terminology and the hiddenness of extended search and filter features. That the tool was designed under those principles and still exhibits both is a useful, candid result: the principles name the right hazards without guaranteeing immunity to them.

They \emph{qualify} the graph-visualisation strand in a way that bears on its premises. That literature, from concept mapping and \emph{ViCurriAS}~\cite{zucker_vicurrias_2009} through \emph{STOPS}~\cite{auvinen_stops_2014} to the elective-focused graph of Trippel and Röpke~\cite{trippel_developing_2025}, is built on the assumption that a curriculum carries a prerequisite structure worth visualising; \emph{STOPS} decomposes courses into prerequisite-linked outcomes, and Trippel and Röpke distinguish required from recommended edges. The curriculum in this study largely does not carry that structure. Outside the introductory phase and ordering within a module, the regulations specify little sequencing, which is why the implemented graph is dominated by containment and carries a prerequisite overlay of two edges, and why a prerequisite advisory arose only once in twenty-two runs of planning. The observation generalises beyond this artefact: where a curriculum encodes few hard dependencies, a dependency-oriented visualisation has little to show, and the value of a curriculum graph shifts from revealing sequence to supporting orientation and filtered browsing. That is what students used it for here, and the substitution result of Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation} says so quantitatively rather than impressionistically.

They \emph{extend} the AIStudyBuddy line~\cite{roepke_study_2024, judel_supporting_2023, trippel_developing_2025} in two ways. First, the closely related result that a graph-based curriculum visualisation is well received~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} is reproduced here at the level of affect but qualified at the level of use: the same graph functioned as a query surface that displaced the catalogue rather than as a planning surface, and the participants who read it most fluently judged it substitutable by its own filters. Second, where that line separates a student application from institution-facing analytics~\cite{roepke_study_2024}, this study contributes a student-side, in-workflow account that institutional analytics cannot surface, namely how the completion signal and the recommendations are experienced while a plan is being formed, including the felt-versus-actual completion gap and the boundary effect described in Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation}.

They give the dashboard critique of Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} a counterpart at the level of the individual student. Caulfield's objection to Course Signals~\cite{caulfield2013} was that its retention result was read as an outcome the signal had produced when the analysis had not established that it had: the signal was credited beyond what had been checked. The completion finding here has the same shape at a smaller scale. The Dashboard reported no missing requirements at the end of every run, students read that as completion, and in eight runs it was not, because the report covered the curriculum's rules and not the task's. Parasuraman and Riley describe the general hazard as misuse of automation, over-reliance that produces failures of monitoring or biased decisions, and name the saliency of the automation's state indicators among the factors that govern it~\cite{parasuraman1997humans}. A completion checklist is an automation of exactly that kind. It is salient, continuous and authoritative, and it does not say what it has not checked, so the student who trusts it has no cue that anything remains to be monitored. The parallel is not a claim about Course Signals, whose signal and setting differ; it is that a trusted signal in an educational dashboard invites the same over-reliance whatever it measures, and that the remedy the finding points to is not a signal trusted less but a signal that is complete.

They also speak to the integration Bodily and Verbert asked for. Of the 93 student-facing systems in their review, only 17\,\% (sixteen) combined a recommendation component with a feedback or visualisation component, and they call for systems that address both what to do, through recommendations, and why, through feedback~\cite{bodily_review_2017}. This tool is one such system, and the study shows how the combination was used rather than whether it outperforms either part. The two components were used together but not as one. The Dashboard drove the loop for every participant (E-G15, 11/11); the Recommendation Panel accompanied it, used by ten of eleven (E-G02, 10/11) but entered a fraction as often as the catalogue or the graph, and set aside by seven participants once they could judge the curriculum for themselves (E-N05, 7/11). The why and the what were consulted in separate places and at separate moments, the checklist to learn what was missing and the recommendations, for some, to learn what might fill it. That is evidence that the combination is used, and used with a division of labour that follows curriculum confidence; it is not evidence that it works better than either component alone, which the design of this study cannot test.

Finally, the substitution result is an instance of cognitive fit~\cite{vessey1991cognitive}. Vessey's theory holds that problem-solving performance depends on the match between representation and task: spatial tasks, those that require perceiving relationships among data, are served by graphs, while symbolic tasks, those that require extracting or acting on discrete values, are served by tables, and a mismatch costs a mental transformation. The hybrid interface makes that distinction physical. Turning an open requirement into a candidate set is a task of relationships, which modules contain which courses and which of those satisfy a named gap, and participants did it in the graph, which they entered at the query step and left at its end. Committing a candidate to a semester is a task of discrete values, this course, this lane, this load against this cap, and participants did it in the table, which alone displays those values. The theory predicts the seam and the study observed it, as the catalogue's displacement on one side and the commit step's return to the table on the other. What the theory does not predict, and the study adds, is that fit can be defeated by what a view fails to display: the table fits the commit task because it shows the semester dimension, and the graph fails it for the same reason, not because a graph could not be made to show it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

The evaluation took one direction through a tool that admits many, and its limitations are those of that direction rather than of the artefact. Most of them are the known price of a decision made on purpose, to study eleven students at the level of every frame rather than a cohort at the level of a questionnaire, and each is stated with what it costs. They bound what the findings can be read to mean; the directions not taken are the subject of Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}).

\paragraph{Study design.}
The study makes no causal claim about the graph. The two scenarios differ simultaneously in the graph's availability, in the persona and the brief, and in presentation order, and the order was fixed by design (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}), so graph availability and second exposure are collinear. Of the process measures reported, only the displacement of the catalogue is attributed to the graph, because neither practice nor a change of topic explains abandoning a working catalogue for a filter panel that did not exist in the first run; the rise in placement rate is reported as a description of the second run and attributed to nothing. The design also partly guarantees the shape of the graph finding. Because the graph was available only after a workflow had formed without it, the observation that it enters an established loop as an excursion is in part a consequence of the design, and the claim is scoped accordingly: where a structural view is introduced into an already-practised planning workflow, it is used for querying rather than committing, and it displaces the surface that previously served the query step. That is arguably the realistic case, since students commonly learn a tool and then discover its further features, but how a student who met the graph first would plan is a separate condition, which Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}) treats as such.

\paragraph{Sample.}
Eleven analysed sessions from two computing programmes at one institution bound generalisation, and bear on the reading of the graph in particular: computing students are unusually fluent with node--link representations, so a general student population would if anything find the graph harder rather than easier, and the value the study locates in the graph's filters rather than its drawing may be the more transferable part. The patterns that rest on subsets of the sample are weaker still. The expertise-moderated use of recommendations and the observation that the graph helped some participants and hindered others rest on few and in places single-participant contrasts with unmeasured, confounded moderators, and are framed as hypotheses for a powered study rather than as findings.

\paragraph{Ecological validity.}
The sessions were moderated by the author, who designed and built the tool and coded the sessions afterwards, and participants knew this. Self-report under those conditions is exposed to demand characteristics, which is one reason the questionnaire means sit near the ceiling of both instruments and are read as the weakest evidence in the chapter; a master's thesis cannot separate the roles, and the observational record is the compensation, since what a participant did on screen is not a courtesy to the moderator. The moderator also resolved operational and conceptual difficulties in every session, unblocked task-blocking issues in several, and ended four scenario runs, so task outcomes are facilitator-qualified rather than independent. For the four most recent participants the extent of that assistance cannot be quantified, because their recordings are screen shares without the webcam tile and their transcripts carry a single speaker label. Planning under a brief, with a persona and a time limit, is also not planning one's own degree; the loop and the substitution are observations about the tool under a task, and their transfer to unprompted use is an open question.

\paragraph{Instrumentation and analysis.}
The acceptance items are TAM-derived rather than the validated TAM: three items per construct rather than six, reworded to the planning domain and answered on an agreement scale, with one ease-of-use item that measures a task outcome and is reported separately for that reason (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-questionnaire}); no reliability claim is made for the shortened scales. The frame sampling that carries the process account has the limits stated in Section~\ref{sec:eval-framemethod}: a ten-second interval misses anything shorter, so counts of transient banners are lower bounds; a frame shows a state and not a reason; and the two recording cohorts differ, seven sessions captured directly at 15 to 30 minutes per scenario against four captured as screen shares at 6 to 12 minutes and lower resolution, so a frequency that fell as a proportion across the full cohort may reflect session length rather than the interface, and lane values in the second cohort are reconciled against the Dashboard total rather than read from the header. The analysis rests on two records, the recording and the transcript, and neither captures intent that was neither spoken nor visible. Coding, adjudication between the records and severity rating were performed by one researcher. The absent check is a second reader rather than a reliability statistic, since Braun and Clarke do not recommend inter-coder reliability measures for the codebook thematic analysis used here~\cite[pp.~236, 241]{braun_thematic_2022}; severities remain provisional single-evaluator judgements.

\section{Generalisability}
\label{sec:disc-generalisability}

A generalisability question specific to the artefact concerns how much of the design is tied to its evaluation context. The curriculum abstraction underlying the tool (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:curriculum-abstraction}) was read directly off TU~Wien's Bachelor Informatics and Master Software Engineering curriculum regulations rather than designed as an institution-agnostic model. Separating what would transfer by re-parameterisation from what would need redesign gives a checkable split. Programme-agnostic are the four-level hierarchy of programme, exam subject, module and course; the plan model and the two views' division of labour; the checklist-driven loop and the Parking Stage; and the per-semester credit limits, which are the tool's own plan-sanity limits rather than curriculum law. Bound to the two TU~Wien programmes, and encoded as one rule set each (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance}), are the two obligation taxonomies, the Bachelor's StEOP course sets and its allowance of credits before the gate, its seven focus areas and their alias table, its variant-mixing rule and its transferable-skills cap, and the Master's core sets per exam subject, its subject-modules minimum, its advanced-topics prefixes and its two enforced prerequisite pairs. A third programme therefore means a third rule set, not a re-parameterisation, and how much of the rule sets could be shared is the question Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance} leaves to that point.

More broadly, the thesis's separable outputs, stated as contributions in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}), generalise differently. The requirements specification and the process findings are informative for study-planning tools in general. The hybrid-specific findings, namely the graph's position in the planning cycle, its dependence on filters, and the Parking Stage's function as a hinge, are evidence for designing hybrid interfaces specifically, not evidence that a hybrid is the right choice for every planner.
