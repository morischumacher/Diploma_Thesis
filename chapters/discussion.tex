\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter interprets the evaluation findings of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, relates them to the prior work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}, records the requirements the study leaves unmet, states the limitations that bound the findings, and outlines future work. Throughout, it distinguishes what the study \emph{establishes} from what it \emph{suggests}: with a small, single-institution sample and a design that was not built to isolate the graph, several of the most interesting observations are hypotheses the study generates rather than effects it confirms, and they are marked as such.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:disc-rq-answers}

Before interpreting the findings, this section states plainly what each research question was answered with, and how completely.

\paragraph{Research Question 1: students' needs and the requirements that follow.}
The answer is the specification of Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}: thirteen features carrying fifty-five numbered requirements, each traceable through a theme to the interview excerpts that motivated it, and ranked by an importance score that governed what was built first. It is answered on the evidence of six students at one faculty, which bounds it in the way Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} states: the specification documents what this population needed, not what students in general need. Two parts of the question are answered less completely than the rest. The needs concerning course dependencies were elicited and specified (Feature~\ref{feat:003}, Req.~2 and~3), but the curricula in scope turned out to encode very little sequence to act on (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:graph-ltr}), so the requirement is met unevenly: Req.~2, the formal relation, is met in the compliance layer and in the graph, on two pairs, while Req.~3, the recommended relation, was met in the compliance layer alone in the system that was evaluated. And the specification is a statement of needs rather than a validated instrument: it was not tested against an alternative elicitation, and four of its requirements were not carried into the implemented system, which the delineations of Features~\ref{feat:005} and~\ref{feat:013} record.

\paragraph{Research Question 2: how such an interface can be designed and implemented.}
The answer is the artefact and, more usefully, the reasoning recorded alongside it. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} answers the design half by deriving each decision from a requirement, a prototype assumption, published guidance, or a stated design judgement, and by naming the alternative it was weighed against where one was live; Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} answers the implementation half with a working system whose central structural decision, a single authoritative plan model from which both views and all feedback are derived, is what makes the two views projections of one plan rather than two plans to reconcile. The question asks \emph{how} such an interface can be built, and a single realisation cannot establish that this is the only way or the best way to build one; what it establishes is that it can be built, and at what cost in specific decisions.

\paragraph{Research Question 3: how students experience the interface and its recommendations.}
This is the question the thesis answers most fully, and Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} answers it along each of the three dimensions the question names. Effectiveness: students completed plans that the tool certified and that frequently did not meet their brief, so the honest answer is that they were effective at the constraints the tool models and unreliable at the two it does not. Efficiency: the process is a single checklist-driven loop whose period does not change with the graph, and the graph substitutes for the catalogue at the query step rather than adding one. Satisfaction: high and consistent, with pragmatic quality above hedonic, and with perspicuity the weakest scale, which matches the terminology and learnability problems the observational record shows. The recommendations half of the question is answered more narrowly than the interface half: the panel was measured and its use characterised, but two of its six families could not fire during the study (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-recommender}), so what was evaluated is the recommendation \emph{workflow} over four working families, not the value of the family set the design defines.

\section{Interpretation of Findings}
\label{sec:disc-interpretation}

\paragraph{The primary result is a process, not a feature.}
The clearest answer to RQ3 is that students converged on one stable way of working, and that this loop, rather than any single component, is what made the tool usable. The pre-fill seeds a plan, the requirement checklist drives it, the recommendations propose candidates, the parking stage stages them, and the table commits them. Three properties make this more than a description of the interface. First, the loop is the same for the fluent, unassisted participants and for the most junior participant who needed to be shown where it started, so it is a property of the artefact rather than of facilitation. Second, it is measurable: its two-station cycle accounts for roughly 40~\% of all observed state changes in both conditions, and its period is unchanged by the availability of the graph. Third, the loop externalises the parts of programme planning that students cannot hold in their heads, namely which requirements remain open and what would close them. This reframes what it means to say the tool works: it works because the guidance surfaces carry the bookkeeping, allowing students to spend their attention on choosing rather than on tracking.

\paragraph{The parking stage is the mechanism that makes a two-view plan coherent.}
A hybrid interface over a shared plan raises an obvious question: how does work discovered in one view reach the other without either view having to duplicate the other's function? The evaluation answers it concretely. The parking stage is a staging buffer that sits outside the plan's accounting, and it is where courses found in the graph, or surfaced by a recommendation, wait until they are assigned in the table. Every participant used it this way. We regard this as the study's most transferable structural finding: a shared-plan hybrid does not need each view to be able to do everything, but it does need an explicit place for a decision that has been made but not yet committed. The same property has a cost, in that parked credits are invisible to every completion readout, which several participants had to compensate for mentally.

\paragraph{The graph occupies a place in the process rather than adding one.}
The most precise result of the evaluation is positional. When the graph becomes available it does not extend the planning cycle with a third station; it takes over the station the catalogue previously held. Catalogue occupancy falls from 27.1~\% to 6.7~\% of sampled frames while the graph accounts for 26.6~\%, the catalogue is entered forty times across the graph-off runs against forty-two graph entries across the graph-on runs, and the probability of moving from the working state to the catalogue falls to zero. The cycle keeps its shape and its period. What changes is the surface on which a named requirement is turned into candidate courses.

This is a stronger statement than the observation that participants liked the graph, and it is more useful for design. It says that the contribution of a hybrid structural view, in this tool and this curriculum, is to the \emph{query} step of planning and not to the \emph{commit} step. Read through cognitive fit~\cite{vessey1991cognitive}, a representation aids performance when it matches the task, and the substitution locates the seam precisely: allocation across semesters is a sequential-scheduling task the table's lanes fit, whereas turning an open requirement into a candidate set is a filtering and orientation task that a browsable hierarchy fits better than a scrollable list.

\paragraph{What the graph did not change is as informative as what it did.}
The share of placement attempts ending in a rejection does not move consistently between conditions: it falls for seven participants and rises for four, which is what chance produces ($p = 0.55$). The trial-and-error loop at the commit stage therefore survives the introduction of the graph untouched. This is coherent with the positional finding rather than in tension with it. The rework arises because term parity and per-lane load are not shown on the semester lane at the moment of placement, which is a property of the table; a surface that improves how candidates are found cannot repair how they are committed.

\paragraph{Placement returns to the table because of what the graph does not display.}
Substantive plan changes were made inside the graph by participants across the experience range, so the graph is not a read-only inspector. The asymmetry that holds is narrower: semester lanes and their ECTS totals are not rendered in graph view at any zoom level, so a student placing a course from a node cannot see the load consequence of the placement. One participant breached a scenario's reduced-load cap this way and discovered it only after switching back, and two named the missing information as their reason for not placing from the graph at all. The division of labour between the two views is therefore not primarily a matter of preference or of representational fit; it follows from the fact that only one of the views shows the constraint being acted against. Stated generally, a second view can be committed from only if it carries enough of the plan's state to show the consequence of the commitment.

\paragraph{The value attributed to the graph was largely the filters.}
The experienced participants who read the graph fluently judged the node--link view close to redundant and attributed what they gained in the graph-on condition to the filters, which are separable from the visualisation and which the table lacks. That reading is consistent with the study's most universal complaint, and with a behaviour visible throughout the recordings: participants entered a view several of them found hard to read in order to reach a control they found essential. One participant switched into the graph for the single purpose of finding a course of a given ECTS value, a query the catalogue cannot express.

\paragraph{The graph rendered containment and formal sequence, but not recommended sequence.}
The distinction that matters here runs through the prerequisite relation rather than around it. Its formal half, the pairs the curriculum itself fixes, was both blocked by the compliance engine and drawn in the graph; its soft half, the recommended orderings the engine holds as non-blocking advisories, had no representation in either view. What the evaluated graph could therefore show of sequence was two edges across 101 courses. The claim that the graph makes dependencies legible is evidenced for two of eleven participants (E-G08), one advisory of this kind fired across the twenty-two runs and none was captured in the 2{,}179 sampled frames, and four participants asked for ordering information and did not obtain it (E-P44). This bounds the chapter's claim: the evaluation speaks to the contribution of a browsable structural view whose dependency overlay was very nearly empty, and cannot speak to the contribution of a view that renders sequence richly, because no participant was shown one.

\paragraph{A user-supplied compliance layer checks the wrong thing for the users who do not supply it.}
Most participants adapted the profile's workload thresholds to their scenario's stated band before or during planning, and the engine then enforced what they had set. Those who did not were checked against the tool's defaults instead, and in one case a scenario was completed with a semester well outside the stated band while the dashboard reported no warning. The setting is discoverable and it works; the design consequence is that a rule-checker parameterised by the user silently mis-checks for exactly the students least likely to parameterise it, who are also the students least able to notice. The complementary asymmetry compounds it: across all twenty-two runs the upper bound blocks with a rejection while the lower bound only advises, so an under-filled semester is never forced to correct.

\paragraph{Explainable recommendations are scaffolding whose value scales inversely with domain confidence (exploratory).}
The recommendation panel was used differently in a patterned way: participants who could not yet judge the curriculum leaned on it as a substitute for domain knowledge, while those who knew the curriculum browsed and then declined it in favour of the explicit checklist and catalogue search, despite the engine visibly responding to their stated interests. This suggests the explainable recommendation is most valuable to the least-expert user and that confident users want a different route to the same end. Given the sample, we offer this as an exploratory pattern rather than an established effect, but its design consequence is clear and is stated below.

\paragraph{Self-report measured felt completion, and the checklist's boundary is where plans failed.}
Every one of the eleven participants rated their Scenario~A performance a success, and no Scenario~A run met the stated constraints. The tool's own completion signal ran ahead of the observed state in the same way, reporting no missing requirements at the end of all twenty-two runs. This is a methodological reason to treat the recording as the effectiveness measure rather than a headline failure of the tool. The instructive case is sharper than a general say-do gap. One participant created an empty semester lane specifically to serve as the mandated internship semester, and then filled it because the checklist reported missing ECTS and knew nothing about internships. A constraint the student had represented himself was invisible to the mechanism he was following, so following the mechanism removed it. The lesson is not that students over-report; it is that a checklist students trust as their compass must cover every constraint they are held to, because whatever it omits is what gets optimised away.

\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:disc-implications}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Put the filters in the surface where work happens.} Placement occurs in the table, and the filter set exists only beside the graph. The substitution result gives this implication its force: if the graph is used as the station at which requirements become candidates, and if what participants valued there is separable from the visualisation, then bringing the same filters into the table and catalogue should deliver much of what the graph-on condition delivered. A testable prediction follows in Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}.
    \item \textbf{Show the constraint in the view where it can be violated.} A view that permits placement must display the load and term consequences of that placement. Rendering semester totals, or at minimum the target lane's state, inside the graph would remove the study's clearest instance of an invisible constraint breach.
    \item \textbf{Repair the commit step in the surface that owns it.} The placement rework loop was untouched by the graph and will remain untouched by any addition to the finding step. Term parity on the lane and valid targets marked before the drop address it directly, using information the tool already computes.
    \item \textbf{Make the completion signal cover the whole task.} Every hard constraint a student is held to should be a first-class, tracked requirement in the checklist, including the reduced-load semester and the per-semester band, which were not modelled. This matters most for the students least able to catch an omission, and it converts the study's most effective surface into a trustworthy signal rather than a partial one.
    \item \textbf{Make status glanceable.} Requirement status behind collapsible panels produced repeated expand-and-collapse round trips, and in four sessions the warnings panel was never opened at all while its counter climbed to five. A warning nobody opens is not a warning.
    \item \textbf{Give a rule-checker safe defaults, not just configurable ones.} Where thresholds are user-supplied, the tool should either derive them from the programme or state plainly which value it is checking against, so that an unconfigured checker does not silently validate against the wrong band, and it should treat a stated range as a range rather than as a single ceiling.
    \item \textbf{Serve both the recommendation route and the checklist-plus-filter route.} Reasoned recommendations help the student without a curriculum model; the confident student reaches for the explicit checklist and a filterable catalogue. Both should be first-class.
\end{itemize}

\section{Requirements Left Unmet}
\label{sec:disc-unmet}

Because the evaluation was analysed from two records per participant, it yields not only problems but a specific inventory of what students asked for and did not have. Table~\ref{tab:unmet} lists the requests that recurred across participants; the full catalogue, with the verbatim request and the surface each belongs to, is in Section~\ref{app:eval-unmet}. Two entries warrant comment.

The first is the reduced-load semester. Both scenarios mandated one, every participant had to produce one, and no participant could designate one: the tool has no semester-level type or cap, so the constraint was satisfied, or not, entirely in the student's head and was never confirmed. This is the requirement whose absence propagated furthest, since it also explains the completion failure discussed above.

The second is prerequisite visibility, and it is not a requirement left undelivered. Feature~\ref{feat:003}, Req.~2 and Req.~3 ask for prerequisite and sequencing constraints to be encoded and reported, and the compliance engine does both: it blocks where the relation is hard and warns where it is advisory, and the Dashboard carries the message. What four participants asked for is narrower and was genuinely absent, namely visibility of the \emph{recommended} half of the relation at the point of decision rather than after the plan has changed (E-P44). The evaluated graph drew the two formal pairs the Bachelor curriculum fixes and nothing beyond them, and only one advisory of this kind fired across the twenty-two runs, which is what a curriculum encoding so little sequence affords. This is therefore a request the evaluation elicited about \emph{where} and \emph{how completely} an existing capability surfaces, not a capability the system lacked; the soft-dependency edge layer built in response is described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} (Section~\ref{sec:impl-prereq-edges}) and was not part of the evaluated system.

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings both confirm and qualify the work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. They \emph{confirm}, in a different tool and sample, two of the problems Bartel et al.~\cite{bartel_design_2024} report for the study-planning assistant they evaluated, namely difficulty with curriculum terminology and the hiddenness of extended search and filter features. That the tool was designed under those principles and still exhibits both is a useful, candid result: the principles name the right hazards without guaranteeing immunity to them.

They \emph{qualify} the graph-visualisation strand in a way that bears on its premises. That literature, from concept mapping and \emph{ViCurriAS}~\cite{zucker_vicurrias_2009} through \emph{STOPS}~\cite{auvinen_stops_2014} to the elective-focused graph of Trippel and Röpke~\cite{trippel_developing_2025}, is built on the assumption that a curriculum carries a prerequisite structure worth visualising; \emph{STOPS} decomposes courses into prerequisite-linked outcomes, and Trippel and Röpke distinguish required from recommended edges. The curriculum in this study largely does not carry that structure. Outside the introductory phase and ordering within a module, the regulations specify very little sequencing, which is why the implemented graph is dominated by containment and carries a prerequisite overlay of two edges, and why a prerequisite advisory arose only once in twenty-two runs of planning. The observation generalises beyond this artefact: where a curriculum encodes few hard dependencies, a dependency-oriented visualisation has little to show, and the value of a curriculum graph shifts from revealing sequence to supporting orientation and filtered browsing. That is what students used it for here, and the substitution result of Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation} says so quantitatively rather than impressionistically.

They \emph{extend} the AIStudyBuddy line~\cite{roepke_study_2024, judel_supporting_2023, trippel_developing_2025} in two ways. First, the closely related result that a graph-based curriculum visualisation is well received~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} is reproduced here at the level of affect but qualified at the level of use: the same graph functioned as a query surface that displaced the catalogue rather than as a planning surface, and the participants who read it most fluently judged it substitutable by its own filters. Second, where that line separates a student application from institution-facing analytics~\cite{roepke_study_2024}, this study contributes a student-side, in-workflow account that institutional analytics cannot surface, namely how the completion signal and the recommendations are experienced while a plan is being formed, including the felt-versus-actual completion gap and the boundary effect described in Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No causal claim about the graph.} The two scenarios differ simultaneously in the graph, the persona content, and presentation order, and the design was not counterbalanced because the study makes no comparative performance claim between them (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}). Of the three process measures reported, only the displacement of the catalogue is attributable to graph availability; the change in placement rate is confounded with practice and is reported as a description of the second run.
    \item \textbf{The design partly guarantees the shape of the graph finding.} Because the graph was available only after a workflow had formed without it, the observation that it enters as an excursion into an established loop is in part a consequence of the design rather than a discovery about the graph. The claim is therefore scoped rather than defended: \emph{where a structural view is introduced into an already-practised planning workflow, it is used for querying rather than committing, and it displaces the surface that previously served the query step}. That remains a real finding, and it is arguably the realistic case, since students commonly learn a tool and then discover its further features rather than meeting everything at once. What the design cannot address is how a student who met the graph first would plan, which Section~\ref{sec:disc-future} treats as a separate condition rather than as the same one reversed.
    \item \textbf{Facilitator assistance in every session.} A moderator resolved operational and conceptual difficulties in every session, and in several cases unblocked task-blocking issues; four scenario runs were ended by the moderator rather than by the participant. Task outcomes are therefore facilitator-qualified rather than independent. For the four most recent participants the extent of that assistance cannot be quantified at all, because their recordings are screen shares with the webcam tile cropped and their transcripts carry a single speaker label, so moderator and participant speech are not separable.
    \item \textbf{Two recording cohorts.} Seven sessions were captured as direct screen recordings of 15 to 30 minutes per scenario; four were captured as screen shares within a video call, of 6 to 12 minutes per scenario and at lower effective resolution. Shorter sessions give a problem less opportunity to appear, so a frequency that fell as a proportion across the full cohort may reflect session length rather than the interface. Lane values in the second cohort are reconciled arithmetically against the dashboard total rather than read from the lane header.
    \item \textbf{Small, computing-heavy sample.} Eleven analysed sessions from computing programmes at a single institution. This bounds generalisation and bears specifically on the reading of the graph: computing students are unusually fluent with node--link representations, so a general student population would if anything find the graph harder rather than easier.
    \item \textbf{Two records, but only two.} The analysis rests on the screen recording and the think-aloud transcript. Neither captures intent that was neither spoken nor visible, and the open-exploration and debrief phases were not covered by the frame sampling in every session, so several codes rest on transcript evidence alone.
    \item \textbf{Single-researcher analysis.} Coding, adjudication between the two records, and severity rating were performed by one researcher without a second coder or formal inter-rater reliability. Severities are provisional single-evaluator judgements. Applying the final codebook to all participants removes the ordering artefact in the frequencies but does not substitute for an independent coder.
    \item \textbf{Instrumentation.} The application's per-scenario timer froze while its browser tab was backgrounded and is not used; in three sessions the recording ended or froze while work continued, so a final observed frame can understate what was achieved. Ten-second sampling also means transient banners shorter than the interval may be missed, so counts of such events are lower bounds.
    \item \textbf{The acceptance instrument is TAM-derived, not the validated TAM.} Three items per construct rather than six, reworded to the planning domain and answered on an agreement rather than a likelihood scale; one ease-of-use item measures a task outcome rather than a cost of operation and is reported separately for that reason (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-questionnaire}). No reliability claim is made for the shortened scales.
    \item \textbf{Exploratory patterns.} The expertise-moderated use of recommendations, and the observation that the graph appeared to help some participants and hinder others, rest on few and in places single-participant contrasts with unmeasured, confounded moderators. They are framed as hypotheses for a powered study.
\end{itemize}

\section{Generalisability}
\label{sec:disc-generalisability}

A generalisability question specific to the artefact concerns how much of the design is tied to its evaluation context. The curriculum abstraction underlying the tool (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:curriculum-abstraction}) was read directly off TU~Wien's Bachelor Informatics and Master Software Engineering curriculum regulations rather than designed as an institution-agnostic model. It is worth separating which parts would need genuine redesign to transfer from which could merely be re-parameterised. The planning workflow, the requirement-checklist-driven loop, the parking stage, and the division of labour between the two views appear largely programme-agnostic and re-parameterisable. The obligation taxonomy, namely mandatory, narrow and broad electives, focus areas, and StEOP-style introductory phases, and the specific compliance rules are the parts most tightly bound to the TU~Wien curriculum and most likely to need rework.\todo{Moritz: expand this with the specifics from the Design chapter's curriculum-abstraction section, exactly which entities and rules are hard-coded to the two TU Wien programmes, so the redesign-versus-reparameterise split is concrete rather than illustrative.}

More broadly, the thesis's separable outputs generalise differently, as noted in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} (Section~\ref{sec:expected-results}). The requirements specification and the process findings are informative for study-planning tools in general. The hybrid-specific findings, namely the graph's position in the planning cycle, its dependence on filters, and the parking stage's function as a hinge, are evidence for designing hybrid interfaces specifically, not evidence that a hybrid is the right choice for every planner.

A final point concerns the artefact rather than the findings. The evaluation reported here takes one direction through a tool that admits several. It asks what a hybrid structural view contributes to the planning process, and it is bounded in that direction by the time available for a single thesis. The same implemented system supports evaluation along other axes that this work does not pursue: the quality and acceptance of the recommendation channels considered on their own, the effect of the compliance layer on plan correctness under controlled prior knowledge, longitudinal use across a real enrolment period rather than a laboratory session, and comparison against the institutional tools students currently use. The tool is therefore best understood as a platform on which a programme of evaluation can be built, of which this study is the first direction and not the only available one.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:disc-future}

Five directions follow directly from the findings.

First, the study yields a falsifiable design hypothesis it could not itself test. If the value participants attributed to the graph-on condition is carried by the filters rather than by the node--link view, then bringing the full filter set into the table should render a separate graph mode largely optional without loss to the planning experience. The substitution result sharpens the prediction: with filters in the table, the query station should stay in the table rather than migrating to the graph, and the graph's occupancy should fall towards the catalogue's current level in the graph-on condition.

Second, a graph-first condition is a genuinely different experiment rather than this one reversed, and naming what it would test clarifies what the present design did and did not buy. Two hypotheses are worth stating. With the graph present during initial learning, it may be folded into the base loop rather than remaining an excursion, in which case the query station would never be established in the catalogue at all. Alternatively, on the evidence of the most junior participant, it may overload a student who has no simpler fallback workflow to retreat to. Both are testable in a powered study and neither is answerable at this sample size.

Third, the unmet requirements of Section~\ref{sec:disc-unmet} constitute a concrete development agenda, of which the reduced-load semester, the enforced workload band, and the per-lane term indicator are the three whose absence most visibly shaped the observed process. The third of these is also the one change predicted to move the placement-rework measure, which the graph left unchanged.

Fourth, prerequisite visibility now has an artefact to test rather than a proposal to weigh. The soft-dependency edge layer described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} (Section~\ref{sec:impl-prereq-edges}) was built after the study and adds the recommended orderings to the formal pairs the evaluated graph already drew; because even together these amount to a handful of edges in each programme, the question it raises is not whether students can read prerequisite edges but whether so few of them change how a plan is built. Two things would be worth establishing together. The first is whether the layer alters the placement-rejection measure, which the graph itself left untouched (Section~\ref{sec:eval-accuracy}) and which the four participants of E-P44 were in effect asking about. The second is the prior data question: the eligibility gates, StEOP and core-before-elective, are what these curricula actually encode, and they are conditions rather than course-to-course edges, so a view that renders gates may serve the same need better than one that renders sequence. Which sequencing information is worth capturing, and from where, is the question to settle before a dependency-revealing view can be said to earn its place.

Fifth, the observations offered here as exploratory, in particular the expertise-moderated use of recommendations, warrant a powered study with an explicit measure of the relevant individual difference and a sample drawn beyond computing programmes.
