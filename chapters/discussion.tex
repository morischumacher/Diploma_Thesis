\section{The Design's Assumptions, Revisited}
\label{sec:disc-assumptions}

The design of Chapter~\ref{chap:design} rested on assumptions it could not test, some inherited from the formative study and some made as design judgements. This section states, for each, what the evaluation now says about it. The arguments are made once, in Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation}; here each assumption gets its verdict.

\paragraph{Graph for exploration, table for commitment.}
Held, and narrower than its wording. The graph served the query step and the table the commit step for every participant who used both (Section~\ref{sec:eval-graphrole}), but what students did in the graph was directed querying, turning a named gap into a candidate set, rather than the open-ended browsing the formative study called exploration (Section~\ref{sec:mutual-exclusion}).

\paragraph{A staging area between finding and committing.}
Held, and more important than the design claimed. Every participant shortlisted before committing (E-G06, 11/11), and the Parking Stage became the mechanism by which work found in one view reached the other; its cost, parked credits invisible to every completion readout (E-P38, 10/11), was not anticipated.

\paragraph{The Dashboard as the student's compass.}
Held beyond its intention. The missing-requirements list drove the entire planning process (E-G15, 11/11), and because participants met the curriculum only through it (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}), what it did not model did not exist for them. The one constraint of both briefs it cannot model, the reduced-load semester, accounts for half of the runs that missed their brief (Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}).

\paragraph{Guide rather than block.}
Held for the rules it covered (E-G04, 11/11), with two consequences the design did not intend: a floor that advises where the ceiling blocks (E-P35, 10/11), and thresholds set by the student rather than by the programme, so that the checker enforced the task only for those who had configured it (Section~\ref{sec:eval-config}).

\paragraph{Recommendations with reasons, beside the plan.}
Half held. Reasoned suggestions were read and used by ten of eleven participants, but they did not drive the process: those who could judge the curriculum for themselves browsed and returned to the checklist (E-N05, 7/11), and the reasons named a relationship without naming the input that had produced it (E-P68, 4/9).

\paragraph{An overlay that draws only what the curriculum fixes.}
Held as a rule of legibility, and met a curriculum with almost nothing to be legible about: two fixed pairs, an overlay nearly empty, and four participants asking for ordering information at the point of placement (E-P44, 4/11). Section~\ref{sec:disc-related} draws the consequence.

\paragraph{The two upstream assumptions.}
On the hybrid concept (Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions}), the evaluation says only that the two surfaces served different steps of one loop and that seven participants named that division as the value of having both (E-N01, 7/11); no non-hybrid alternative was run, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}) states the open question. On what a curriculum encodes, the finding is the curriculum's rather than the evaluation's: the regulations in scope publish almost no sequencing, and students used the graph for what such a curriculum affords, orientation and filtered browsing.

\section{Interpretation of Findings}
\label{sec:disc-interpretation}

\paragraph{The primary result is a process, not a feature.}
The primary answer to RQ3 is that participants converged on one stable loop, and that this loop, rather than any single component, is what made the tool usable. The pre-fill seeds a plan, the checklist drives it, the recommendations, the catalogue and the graph propose candidates, the Parking Stage holds them, and the table commits them. Three properties make this more than a description of the interface. The loop is the same for the fluent, unassisted participants and for the most junior participant who had to be shown where it started, so it is a property of the artefact rather than of facilitation. It is measurable: its two-station cycle accounts for roughly 40~\% of all observed state changes in both conditions, and its period is unchanged by the graph. And it externalises the part of programme planning students cannot hold in their heads, which requirements remain open and what would close them, so that attention goes to choosing rather than to tracking.

\paragraph{The tool was the curriculum.}
Because the briefs stated no curriculum rules, participants met them only through the Dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:eval-conditions}). For the duration of a task the tool did not support an understanding of the curriculum; it constituted it. This is the general form of several specific findings: terminology the tool did not define stayed unclear (E-P01), a requirement the tool did not model was optimised away, and the two least confident participants used the Dashboard and the recommendations as a substitute for curriculum knowledge (E-N06). A tool that is the student's only channel to the rules inherits the obligation to be complete, which is stronger than the obligation to be correct.

\paragraph{The Parking Stage is the mechanism that makes a two-view plan coherent.}
A hybrid interface over one plan has to move work between views without either view duplicating the other. The Parking Stage is how this tool does it: a buffer outside the plan's accounting where courses found in the graph, or surfaced by a recommendation, wait until they are assigned in the table, and every participant used it that way. This is the study's most transferable structural finding, that a shared-plan hybrid does not need each view to do everything, but does need an explicit place for a decision made and not yet committed. The same property is its cost, since parked credits are invisible to every completion readout and several participants compensated for that in their heads.

\paragraph{The graph occupies a place in the process rather than adding one.}
The most precise result of the evaluation is positional. When the graph becomes available it does not extend the planning cycle with a third station; it takes over the station the catalogue held, at the catalogue's occupancy and entry count, and the cycle keeps its shape and its period (Section~\ref{sec:eval-substitution}). What changes is the surface on which a named requirement is turned into candidate courses. This is a stronger statement than that participants liked the graph, and a more useful one for design: the contribution of a hybrid structural view, in this tool and this curriculum, is to the query step of planning and not to the commit step. Section~\ref{sec:disc-related} reads that seam through cognitive fit.

\paragraph{Where the graph's value sat: the query step, and largely its filters.}
The participants who read the graph most fluently attributed what they gained in the graph-on condition to the filter sidebar rather than to the node--link drawing (E-G07, 11/11; E-P04, 11/11), and the recordings agree: participants entered a view several of them found hard to read in order to reach a control they found essential, and one switched into the graph for the single purpose of finding a course of a given ECTS value, a query the catalogue cannot express. The value of the graph-on condition therefore sits largely in a component separable from the visualisation. The graph is not thereby worthless, since interest-driven browsing (E-G10, 9/11) and module composition (E-G17, 6/11) are things the filters do not give; but the study cannot tell how much of its contribution would survive if the table had the filters, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}) states that as the hypothesis it is.

\paragraph{Placement returns to the table because of what the graph does not display.}
Plan changes were made inside the graph by participants across the experience range, so the graph is not a read-only inspector. The asymmetry that holds is narrower: semester lanes and their totals are not rendered in Graph View at any zoom level, so a student placing from a node cannot see the load consequence of the placement. One participant breached a reduced-load cap this way and discovered it only after switching back, and two named the missing information as their reason for not placing from the graph at all. Stated generally, a second view can be committed from only if it carries enough of the plan's state to show the consequence of the commitment.

\paragraph{A user-supplied compliance layer checks the wrong thing for the users who do not supply it.}
Most participants set the profile's workload thresholds to their brief, and the engine then enforced what they had set. Those who did not were checked against the defaults, and two Scenario~B runs ended with a semester above the brief's ceiling and no warning, because the ceiling in force was still the default (Section~\ref{sec:eval-config}). The setting is discoverable and it works; the consequence is that a rule-checker parameterised by the user mis-checks for %% REV (is this proofable that is failes for exactly these ones?)
exactly the students least likely to parameterise it and least able to notice. The asymmetry between a blocking ceiling and an advisory floor compounds it, since an under-filled semester is never asked to correct.
%% REV (this implies all the others are not explorator<? I think the whole study is so)
\paragraph{Explainable recommendations are scaffolding whose value scales inversely with domain confidence (exploratory).}
Participants who could not yet judge the curriculum leaned on the recommendations as a substitute for domain knowledge, while those who knew it browsed and then declined them in favour of the checklist and catalogue search, although the engine visibly responded to their stated interests. The pattern suggests that an explainable recommendation is worth most to the least expert user and that confident users want a different route to the same end. Given the sample it is offered as an exploratory pattern rather than an effect; its design consequence is stated below.

\paragraph{Self-report measured felt completion, and the checklist's boundary is where plans failed.}
Seven runs were rated a success by the participant who produced them and did not meet their brief, and all twenty-two ended with the Dashboard reporting no missing requirements, a report that was correct for the curriculum's requirements and silent on the task's (Section~\ref{sec:eval-effectiveness}). The mechanism matters more than the gap. A student can hold a correct model of the task and lose it, because the model and the checklist are never weighed against each other: the checklist speaks in the interface, continuously and with authority, while the student's own constraint has no representation there and has to be remembered against it. When the two conflict the checklist wins, not because students trust it more than themselves but because it is the only one of the two being read, and a constraint outside its model is traded away in satisfying the constraints inside it. The lesson is not that students over-report; it is that a checklist students treat as their compass must cover every constraint they are held to.

\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:disc-implications}
%% REV (for my these design imppplications are to specific, can we generalize them. These ones just help when somebody want to build the same tool again)
Each implication below is a general rule for a planning interface of this kind, followed by the observation in this study that produced it.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Put the filters in the surface where work happens.} Placement occurs in the table, and the filter set exists only beside the graph. If what participants valued in the graph is separable from the drawing, bringing the same filters into the table and catalogue should deliver much of what the graph-on condition delivered; Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}) states the prediction.
    \item \textbf{Show the constraint in the view where it can be violated.} A view that permits placement must display the load and term consequences of that placement. Rendering semester totals, or at minimum the target lane's state, inside the graph would remove the study's clearest instance of an invisible constraint breach.
    \item \textbf{Repair the commit step in the surface that owns it.} The placement rework loop was untouched by the graph and remains untouched by any addition to the finding step. Term parity on the lane and valid targets marked before the drop address it directly, using information the tool already computes.
    \item \textbf{Make the completion signal cover the whole task.} Every hard constraint a student is held to should be a first-class, tracked requirement in the checklist, including the reduced-load semester and the per-semester band, which were not modelled. This matters most for the students least able to catch an omission, and it converts the study's most effective surface into a trustworthy signal rather than a partial one.
    \item \textbf{Count what is staged.} A holding area is necessary for a two-view plan, but a total that silently excludes it is a wrong total. Parked credits should be visible in every readout that a student reads as completion, either counted separately or flagged as uncounted (E-P38, 10/11).
    \item \textbf{Make status glanceable.} Requirement status behind collapsible panels produced repeated expand-and-collapse round trips, and in four sessions the warnings panel was never opened at all while its counter climbed to five. A warning nobody opens is not a warning.
    \item \textbf{Give a rule-checker safe defaults, not just configurable ones.} Where thresholds are user-supplied, the tool should either derive them from the programme or state plainly which value it is checking against, so that an unconfigured checker does not silently validate against the wrong band, and it should treat a stated range as a range rather than as a single ceiling.
    \item \textbf{Show ordering where the choice is made, not as a verdict afterwards.} The engine can enforce ordering wherever the curriculum states it and had almost nothing to enforce; what four participants asked for was ordering information on the lane or node where a course is about to be placed (E-P44). A constraint the engine checks is a matter of correctness and can come after the fact; knowledge a student needs while choosing is a matter of timing and has to be in the view where the choice is made.
    \item \textbf{Serve both the recommendation route and the checklist-plus-filter route.} Reasoned recommendations help the student without a curriculum model; the confident student reaches for the explicit checklist and a filterable catalogue. Both should be first-class.
\end{itemize}

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings both confirm and qualify the work reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. They \emph{confirm}, in a different tool and sample, two of the problems Bartel et al.~\cite{bartel_design_2024} report for the study-planning assistant they evaluated, namely difficulty with curriculum terminology and the hiddenness of extended search and filter features. That the tool was designed under those principles and still exhibits both is a useful, candid result: the principles name the right hazards without guaranteeing immunity to them.

They \emph{qualify} the graph-visualisation strand in a way that bears on its premises. That literature, from concept mapping and \emph{ViCurriAS}~\cite{zucker_vicurrias_2009} through \emph{STOPS}~\cite{auvinen_stops_2014} to the elective-focused graph of Trippel and Röpke~\cite{trippel_developing_2025}, is built on the assumption that a curriculum carries a prerequisite structure worth visualising; \emph{STOPS} decomposes courses into prerequisite-linked outcomes, and Trippel and Röpke distinguish required from recommended edges. The curriculum in this study largely does not carry that structure. Outside the introductory phase and ordering within a module, the regulations specify little sequencing, which is why the implemented graph is dominated by containment and carries a prerequisite overlay of two edges, and why a prerequisite advisory arose only once in twenty-two runs of planning. The observation generalises beyond this artefact: where a curriculum encodes few hard dependencies, a dependency-oriented visualisation has little to show, and the value of a curriculum graph shifts from revealing sequence to supporting orientation and filtered browsing. That is what students used it for here.

They \emph{extend} the AIStudyBuddy line~\cite{roepke_study_2024, judel_supporting_2023, trippel_developing_2025} in two ways. First, the closely related result that a graph-based curriculum visualisation is well received~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} is reproduced here at the level of affect but qualified at the level of use: the same graph functioned as a query surface that displaced the catalogue rather than as a planning surface, and the participants who read it most fluently judged it substitutable by its own filters. Second, where that line separates a student application from institution-facing analytics~\cite{roepke_study_2024}, this study contributes a student-side, in-workflow account that institutional analytics cannot surface, namely how the completion signal and the recommendations are experienced while a plan is being formed, including the felt-versus-actual completion gap and the boundary effect described in Section~\ref{sec:disc-interpretation}.

They give the dashboard critique of Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} a counterpart at the level of the individual student. Caulfield's objection to Course Signals~\cite{caulfield2013} was that its retention result was read as an outcome the signal had produced when the analysis had not established that it had: the signal was credited beyond what had been checked. The completion finding here has the same shape at a smaller scale. The Dashboard reported no missing requirements at the end of every run, students read that as completion, and in eight runs it was not, because the report covered the curriculum's rules and not the task's. Parasuraman and Riley describe the general hazard as misuse of automation, over-reliance that produces failures of monitoring or biased decisions, and name the saliency of the automation's state indicators among the factors that govern it~\cite{parasuraman1997humans}. A completion checklist is an automation of exactly that kind. It is salient, continuous and authoritative, and it does not say what it has not checked, so the student who trusts it has no cue that anything remains to be monitored. The parallel is not a claim about Course Signals, whose signal and setting differ; it is that a trusted signal in an educational dashboard invites the same over-reliance whatever it measures, and that the remedy the finding points to is not a signal trusted less but a signal that is complete.

They also speak to the integration Bodily and Verbert asked for. Of the 93 student-facing systems in their review, only 17\,\% (sixteen) combined a recommendation component with a feedback or visualisation component, and they call for systems that address both what to do, through recommendations, and why, through feedback~\cite{bodily_review_2017}. This tool is one such system, and the study shows how the combination was used rather than whether it outperforms either part. The two components were used together but not as one. The Dashboard drove the loop for every participant (E-G15, 11/11); the Recommendation Panel accompanied it, used by ten of eleven (E-G02, 10/11) but entered a fraction as often as the catalogue or the graph, and set aside by seven participants once they could judge the curriculum for themselves (E-N05, 7/11). The why and the what were consulted in separate places and at separate moments, the checklist to learn what was missing and the recommendations, for some, to learn what might fill it. That is evidence that the combination is used, and used with a division of labour that follows curriculum confidence; it is not evidence that it works better than either component alone, which the design of this study cannot test.

Finally, the substitution result is an instance of cognitive fit~\cite{vessey1991cognitive}. Vessey's theory holds that problem-solving performance depends on the match between representation and task: spatial tasks, those that require perceiving relationships among data, are served by graphs, while symbolic tasks, those that require extracting or acting on discrete values, are served by tables, and a mismatch costs a mental transformation. The hybrid interface makes that distinction physical. Turning an open requirement into a candidate set is a task of relationships, which modules contain which courses and which of those satisfy a named gap, and participants did it in the graph, which they entered at the query step and left at its end. Committing a candidate to a semester is a task of discrete values, this course, this lane, this load against this cap, and participants did it in the table, which alone displays those values. The theory predicts the seam and the study observed it, as the catalogue's displacement on one side and the commit step's return to the table on the other. What the theory does not predict, and the study adds, is that fit can be defeated by what a view fails to display: the table fits the commit task because it shows the semester dimension, and the graph fails it for the same reason, not because a graph could not be made to show it.


\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The empirical findings systematically confirm, qualify, and extend the literature reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}. 

First, the results \emph{confirm} the specific usability friction points identified by Bartel et al.~\cite{bartel_design_2024} regarding study-planning assistants. Despite operating a substantially different interface and sample, participants in this study exhibited identical difficulties deciphering institutional curriculum terminology and discovering nested filtering features. The replication of these friction points within a novel architecture constitutes a valuable empirical finding: applying heuristic design principles identifies known usability hazards but does not inherently guarantee immunity from them.

Second, the findings \emph{qualify} the foundational premise of the graph-visualization literature. Systems such as \emph{ViCurriAS}~\cite{zucker_vicurrias_2009}, \emph{STOPS}~\cite{auvinen_stops_2014}, and the elective visualizations by Trippel and Röpke~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} predicate their design on the existence of a dense, explicit prerequisite network. However, the curricula evaluated in this study fundamentally lack this topological density. Because formal regulations mandate minimal sequential dependencies outside introductory phases, the resulting graph structurally encodes hierarchical containment rather than chronological sequence. Consequently, a dependency-centric visualization yields marginal operational value, evidenced by prerequisite warnings triggering only once across 22 planning runs. This observation generalizes beyond this specific artefact: when a curriculum lacks dense programmatic routing, the utility of a curriculum graph transitions from illustrating rigid sequences to facilitating spatial orientation and filtered browsing.

Third, the results \emph{extend} the AIStudyBuddy lineage~\cite{roepke_study_2024, judel_supporting_2023, trippel_developing_2025} in two distinct directions. Initially, the finding that a graph-based curriculum visualization is well received~\cite{trippel_developing_2025} is reproduced here at the level of affective reception, but explicitly qualified at the level of operational use. While participants appreciated the graph, it functioned strictly as a query surface that displaced the catalogue rather than as a holistic planning surface, with fluent users judging the visualization largely substitutable by its own filter panel. Furthermore, where prior work separates student applications from institution-facing analytics~\cite{roepke_study_2024}, this study contributes an in-workflow, student-side account that institutional metrics cannot capture. Specifically, it reveals how completion signals and recommendations are actually experienced during plan formation, culminating in the %% REV (scientific language??) 
felt-versus-actual completion gap.

This completion gap extends the dashboard critique of Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} by providing a direct counterpart at the individual user level. Caulfield's methodological objection to Course Signals~\cite{caulfield2013} highlighted the danger of institutional reliance on flawed or unverified systemic metrics. The completion illusion observed in this study exhibits the exact same mechanism at a micro scale. Because the Dashboard erroneously reported zero missing requirements, participants trusted this authoritative signal over their own task models. Parasuraman and Riley~\cite{parasuraman1997humans} categorize this general hazard as the \enquote{misuse of automation}, where over-reliance on salient system indicators produces severe monitoring failures. An interface checklist operates as an automation of this exact kind: it is continuous, salient, and authoritative, leaving the trusting student with no cognitive cue that unmodeled constraints require manual monitoring. The parallel confirms that a trusted UI signal invites systematic over-reliance regardless of what it measures, dictating that completion checklists must be exhaustive rather than merely advisory.

These behavioral patterns also respond directly to Bodily and Verbert's call for systemic integration. Of the 93 student-facing systems in their review, only 17\,\% integrated recommendations with visual feedback mechanisms, prompting a call for interfaces that address both \emph{what} to do and \emph{why} to do it~\cite{bodily_review_2017}. While this evaluated tool provides both components simultaneously, the visual frame logs reveal a strict functional compartmentalization. %% REV (do you have to restate the numbers here again or would you cut them?)

Crucially, seven participants explicitly abandoned the recommendations in favor of deterministic checklist searches once they acquired sufficient curriculum confidence (E-N05, 7/11). This provides evidence that the combination is utilized, but with a strict division of labor dictated by domain expertise, where probabilistic suggestions serve primarily as novice scaffolding.

Finally, the displacement of the catalogue by the graph physically manifests Vessey's Cognitive Fit Theory~\cite{vessey1991cognitive}. Vessey posits that problem-solving performance optimizes when the visual representation matches the cognitive task: spatial tasks (perceiving relationships) are served by graphs, whereas symbolic tasks (extracting discrete values) are served by tables. \section{Limitations}

Consequently, the limitations discussed here bound this specific study design rather than the system itself. These constraints reflect deliberate methodological trade-offs, specifically the decision to prioritize deep, frame-by-frame behavioral tracing of a small cohort over shallow, survey-based generalization. \paragraph{Study design.}
The fixed-order sequence precludes strict causal claims regarding the graph. The two scenarios differ simultaneously in graph availability, persona context, task brief, and presentation order (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}). Therefore, graph availability and second-task exposure are collinear. Of the reported process metrics, only the catalogue's displacement is strictly attributed to the graph (Section~\ref{sec:eval-substitution}); the increased placement rate is documented descriptively as a second-run effect. Furthermore, the fixed design inherently shapes the graph finding: because the graph was introduced only after a tabular workflow had formed, the observation that it functions as an excursion from an established loop is partially an artefact of the study design. The claim is scoped accordingly: %% REV (you just say this here in this way, is this okay or do you also have to put it in this context where you make the statement?) 
when a structural view is introduced into a practiced tabular workflow, it serves querying rather than committing. While this arguably reflects high ecological validity (as students typically discover advanced features sequentially), the behavior of a user encountering the graph first constitutes a separate condition reserved for future research.

\paragraph{Sample.}
The evaluation of eleven sessions from two computing programmes at a single institution limits broad generalization. This sample bias specifically impacts the graph findings: computing students exhibit atypical fluency with node-link representations. Consequently, a broader student population would likely find the graph visualization more challenging, suggesting that the most transferable value of the graph-on condition lies in its filter panel rather than its spatial topology. Furthermore, patterns resting on sample subsets (e.g., expertise-moderated recommendation use, or polarizing graph reception) rely on sparse, occasionally single-participant contrasts with unmeasured confounding variables. They are framed as exploratory hypotheses for powered studies rather than established effects.

\paragraph{Ecological validity.}
Because the author operated as both the system designer and the session moderator, self-reported data is highly vulnerable to demand characteristics. This dynamic explains the near-ceiling questionnaire means, which are consequently treated as the weakest evidence in this chapter. While a master's thesis cannot circumvent this dual role, the quantitative frame sampling compensates for it: objective behavioral telemetry (screen interactions) is highly resistant to moderator bias. Additionally, task outcomes are facilitator-qualified rather than independent, as the moderator resolved conceptual difficulties and critical blockages across multiple sessions. Finally, executing a time-boxed persona task diverges cognitively from authentic, self-directed degree planning; translating these task-based behavioral loops to unprompted real-world usage remains an open question.

\paragraph{Instrumentation and analysis.}
The acceptance instrument utilized adapted TAM constructs rather than the validated TAM: it employed three items per construct rather than six, reworded questions for the planning domain, and utilized an agreement scale. One ease-of-use item measuring a task outcome was mathematically isolated to preserve construct validity (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-questionnaire}). No statistical reliability claims are made for these shortened scales. Regarding the visual analysis, the ten-second frame sampling inherently omits sub-interval transient events, meaning counts of transient UI banners represent strict lower bounds. Furthermore, visual frames capture interface state rather than user intent. The recording cohorts also introduce temporal variance: seven sessions ranged from 15 to 30 minutes in duration, whereas the remaining four lasted strictly between 6 and 12 minutes. Therefore, proportional frequency drops may reflect session duration rather than interface interaction. %% REV (was this not already told before as a limitation here in a earlaer item?)
Finally, the analysis relies on a single researcher. While a second independent coder would provide a valuable comparative check, the absence of inter-coder reliability metrics is methodologically consistent with Braun and Clarke, who explicitly recommend against such statistics for the codebook thematic analysis utilized here~\cite[pp.~236, 241]{braun_thematic_2022}. Severity ratings therefore remain provisional, single-evaluator judgements.

\section{Generalisability}
\label{sec:disc-generalisability}

A generalisability question specific to the artefact concerns how much of the design is tied to its evaluation context. The curriculum abstraction underlying the tool (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:curriculum-abstraction}) was read directly off TU~Wien's Bachelor Informatics and Master Software Engineering curriculum regulations rather than designed as an institution-agnostic model. Separating what would transfer by re-parameterisation from what would need redesign gives a checkable split. %% REV (i would split here in generizability of the tool, and generazabilitly of the findings)
Programme-agnostic are the four-level hierarchy of programme, exam subject, module and course; the plan model and the two views' division of labour; the checklist-driven loop and the Parking Stage; and the per-semester credit limits, which are the tool's own plan-sanity limits rather than curriculum law. Bound to the two TU~Wien programmes, and encoded as one rule set each (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance}), are the two obligation taxonomies, the Bachelor's StEOP course sets and its allowance of credits before the gate, its seven focus areas and their alias table, its variant-mixing rule and its transferable-skills cap, and the Master's core sets per exam subject, its subject-modules minimum, its advanced-topics prefixes and its two enforced prerequisite pairs. A third programme therefore means a third rule set, not a re-parameterisation, and how much of the rule sets could be shared is the question Section~\ref{sec:impl-compliance} leaves to that point.

More broadly, the thesis's separable outputs, stated as contributions in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}), generalise differently. The requirements specification and the process findings are informative for study-planning tools in general. The hybrid-specific findings, namely the graph's position in the planning cycle, its dependence on filters, and the Parking Stage's function as a hinge, are evidence for designing hybrid interfaces specifically, not evidence that a hybrid is the right choice for every planner.
