\section{Research Paradigm}
\label{sec:paradigm}

This thesis follows Munzner's nested model for visualisation design and validation~\cite{munzner2009}. The nested model organises a visualisation design process into four stratified levels: (1)~domain problem characterisation, (2)~data and task abstraction, (3)~visual encoding and interaction design, and (4)~algorithm design. A defining property of the model, central to how this thesis is structured, is that each level's output is the next level's input, so that an error or unwarranted assumption at an upstream level cascades to every level downstream of it; consequently, the model calls for validation at each level individually rather than only at the end of the pipeline \cite{munzner2009}.

This thesis's use of the model is: level~1 (domain characterisation) is addressed by the Related Work review (Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}) together with the formative study's needfinding; level~2 (data/task abstraction) is addressed by the codebook-based translation of formative findings into a features-and-requirements specification (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}); level~3 (visual encoding and interaction design) is addressed by the Design chapter; and level~4 (algorithm design) is addressed by the Implementation chapter. Munzner's own level~2 construct is narrower than what Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements} actually produces: the model calls for generic operations and data types (e.g.\ filter, sort, compare, applied to a table or a graph), while this thesis produces a full 13-feature requirements specification. That specification is not a substitute for level~2, the generic operations and data types the model expects are present inside it, just embedded in a richer document rather than isolated on their own. Standard requirements-engineering method is used to build that richer document, nested within this thesis's own level~2 rather than a competing method.

The two empirical studies in this thesis validate different, non-overlapping spans of this model, and it matters exactly which span each one covers. The formative study (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}) validates the needs and requirements derived \emph{within} the graph-table hybrid concept, level~2 primarily, drawing on level~1 needfinding, before design work begins; it does \emph{not} validate the hybrid concept itself, whether a combined graph-and-table interface is the right approach at all remains an assumption carried in from Related Work, stated explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions} rather than tested by this study. The evaluation study (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}) validates the encoding/interaction and algorithm levels, levels~3 and~4, of the resulting artefact, after implementation. This split follows the model's own guidance directly: authors should distinguish between levels when contributions span more than one, and should state upstream assumptions explicitly rather than leave them implicit \cite{munzner2009}. The model is also used later to scope the Design chapter's own contribution explicitly (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}). Figure~\ref{fig:nested-model-mapping} shows this mapping against the process stages introduced in Section~\ref{sec:process-overview}.

Two parts of this work fit the model less directly, and it is worth saying where. Munzner's level~2 does not itself specify how to derive a requirements specification from qualitative interview data, so standard requirements-engineering method fills that gap (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}); and a meaningful share of the Implementation chapter, the compliance rule engine and the database schema, is backend business logic rather than visualisation algorithm design in the narrower sense level~4 usually addresses. Munzner's own paper anticipates this: a single work need not contribute originally at every level, and her worked example relies on a previously proposed abstraction at one level while contributing at others~\cite{munzner2009}. The model is therefore used here as the outermost organising structure, with more specific methods nested inside each level where it is silent, rather than as a competing framework.

\section{Process Overview}
\label{sec:process-overview}

The end-to-end research process spans six stages: (1) a review of related work establishing the domain and justifying a hybrid graph-table interface (Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}); (2) a formative study using an interactive but non-functional prototype to elicit student needs (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}); (3) translation of formative findings into a ranked feature and requirements specification (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}); (4) design of the system, with every design decision traceable to a requirement, prior evidence, or an explicitly stated assumption (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}); (5) implementation of the designed system (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}); and (6) a second, evaluative user study assessing the implemented system against the research questions (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). Each stage's own method is documented in full within its respective chapter; this section states only the umbrella logic connecting them.

The two studies differ enough in conduct to be worth distinguishing here. The formative study was an interview study around an interactive but non-functional prototype, with six participants, and its purpose was elicitation. The evaluation study was an observational study of the working system with eleven analysed participants, and its purpose was to record what students did with it. Each evaluation session followed one fixed sequence administered through a purpose-built study-guide application: consent and demographics, a short video tutorial, the first planning scenario and its questionnaire, the second scenario and its questionnaire, an open-exploration phase, a final user-experience questionnaire, and a semi-structured interview. Participants thought aloud throughout the working phases and a moderator was present for operational questions. Every session was screen- and audio-recorded, and the analysis rests on two records produced independently from those recordings, a transcript and a frame-sampled log of the interface state, which are then triangulated. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} documents the instruments, the labelling scheme, and the coding procedure in full.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:overall-scope}

This thesis defines four system-wide scope boundaries, denoted OOS1 through OOS4 (Out Of Scope). OOS1 and OOS2 are general enough to be justified independently of any specific finding, and are stated here:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OOS1 (no automated planning):} The system guides students in planning; it does not plan on their behalf. This reflects both the intended role of the tool (decision support, not decision automation) and the limited context available to the system to plan responsibly on a student's behalf.
    \item \textbf{OOS2 (programme-level granularity):} Study planning is defined at the programme level throughout this thesis. Course-level planning, scheduling within a single course, deadline management, and appointment tracking are out of scope.
\end{itemize}

Two degree programmes at the Faculty of Informatics of TU~Wien are in scope, and no others: the Bachelor programme in Computer Science (UE~033~521) and the Master programme in Software Engineering \& Internet Computing (UE~066~937). Every curriculum rule the system checks, every catalogue entry it serves, and every plan evaluated in either study belongs to one of these two programmes, and the compliance engine holds a separate rule set for each (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}). The two were chosen because they sit at different stages of a student's path, so that the requirements gathered span both a largely prescribed early curriculum and a largely elective later one.

OOS3 (no live connection to the institutional CMS/LMS) and OOS4 (no community features) are stated later, in Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements} (Section~\ref{sec:overall-scope-refined}), alongside the specific excluded findings that justify them.

\section{Limitations and Constraints}
\label{sec:overall-scope-detail}

Limitations describe how strongly and how broadly the findings in this thesis should be trusted, given how the underlying studies were actually conducted; they are consequences of methodological choices already made, not premises assumed from the outset. Beyond the scope boundaries above, four such limitations bound how the findings in this thesis should be read:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-institution scope.} Both the formative-study participants and the initial system's curriculum data are drawn exclusively from TU Wien's Informatics faculty, covering one Bachelor's and one Master's programme. All six formative-study participants were Informatics students; students with different disciplinary backgrounds, or at other institutions, may have different planning needs not captured here.
    \item \textbf{Small sample sizes in both studies.} The formative study involved six participants (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}); the evaluation study analyses eleven participants, twelve sessions less one that could not be analysed (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}), which is smaller than would support strong statistical generalisation. Both studies are designed for depth of insight within a bounded population, not for population-level generalisability.
    \item \textbf{Limited direct prior research on hybrid graph-table interfaces specifically.} As Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} shows, few existing systems combine a graph and a table view for study planning in exactly this way; the evidence motivating the hybrid concept is inferential, drawn from adjacent systems and general visualisation/HCI literature, rather than from a substantial, converging body of research that directly tests hybrid interfaces of this kind against alternatives.
    \item \textbf{Single-researcher qualitative analysis.} The formative study's coding and theme development was conducted by a single researcher, without a second coder or formal inter-coder reliability check. This is consistent with the choice of codebook TA over coding-reliability TA (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}, Section~\ref{sec:analysis-method}), which does not require multiple coders, but it remains a limitation relative to approaches that do.
\end{itemize}

\section{Expected Results}
\label{sec:expected-results}

This thesis produces three outputs, one per stage of the process described in Section~\ref{sec:process-overview} from the formative study onward: a ranked features-and-requirements specification grounded in interview evidence (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}); a designed and implemented graph-table hybrid realising it, with every design decision traceable to a requirement, a prototype assumption, common design knowledge, or literature (Chapters~\ref{chap:design}--\ref{chap:implementation}); and empirical findings on how that tool performs against the research questions (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). The third takes two forms: a process account derived from the frame-sampled interface record rather than from what participants reported, and a catalogue of usability problems and positive findings, each carried by a code with a participant-level frequency and mapped to an ISO~9241-11 dimension~\cite{iso9241_11}. Both are bounded by a within-subjects design with a fixed scenario order and eleven participants, and therefore support description of a planning process rather than causal attribution to any one interface component. Which of these outputs generalise beyond the hybrid concept, and which are evidence about hybrids specifically, is taken up in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-generalisability}).

\section{Data Handling}
\label{sec:ethics-overview}

Both studies followed the same consent, recording, and anonymisation practice. Participants gave consent before any data collection began, for the formative study as the first phase of each interview session, for the evaluation study via a signed consent and data-processing declaration completed before the session, covering voluntary participation, the right to withdraw at any time without giving a reason and without negative consequences, and, for the evaluation study, the categories of data collected and the legal basis for processing it (Art.~6(1)(a) GDPR). All interviews and sessions were audio- or video-recorded, transcribed, and anonymised, with participants referred to by code (I1--I6 for the formative study; P0xA/P0xB-style codes for the evaluation study) rather than by name throughout all analysis artefacts and this thesis.

The evaluation study's sessions were administered through a purpose-built tool distinct from the study-planning tool this thesis otherwise describes: a lightweight questionnaire web application, developed specifically to present the scenario briefs, administer the post-task instruments, and log responses for each session (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}), separate from and not part of the Design and Implementation artefact evaluated here.

\section{Quality Criteria and Validity Strategy}
\label{sec:validity-overview}

Qualitative rigour for the formative study's analysis is addressed directly in that chapter's Analysis Method section (Section~\ref{sec:analysis-method}), which positions the coding approach explicitly within Braun and Clarke's codebook thematic-analysis tradition and motivates that positioning against alternative approaches (coding-reliability TA, reflexive TA) rather than adopting a method by default. Per the nested-model logic in Section~\ref{sec:paradigm}, validity at the design and implementation levels is addressed through the traceability chain requiring each design decision to cite a requirement, prior evidence, or an explicit assumption (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}), and validity at the outermost level is addressed through the evaluation study (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}).
The evaluation study's own quality strategy rests on separating what is observed from what is reported. Two records are produced per participant and labelled independently of each other, so that a claim can be checked in one against the other rather than resting on a single source; the chapter reports one case in which they disagree, where every participant reported success in a condition in which no plan met its brief, and the observed record is the one that carries the effectiveness claim. The codebook is applied to every participant rather than only to the sessions in which a code was first noticed, so that reported frequencies express how often something was evidenced rather than when it happened to be seen, and denominators below the full cohort mark codes that could not be decided for every participant. Two acknowledged weaknesses remain. Severity ratings are a single-analyst judgement, so the problem ranking is indicative rather than exact. And the acceptance items are a domain-adapted short form rather than the validated Technology Acceptance Model scales, reported with their reliability coefficients and with the ceiling in the responses that produced them, so they are read as descriptive indicators rather than as validated constructs; the User Experience Questionnaire is used unmodified, but eleven participants support description rather than inference.

Drawing these together, the principal threats to validity in this thesis are of three kinds. Internal validity is bounded in the evaluation study by a fixed scenario order, which makes graph availability inseparable from second exposure and from the change of scenario topic, so that chapter reports the substitution result, which the order cannot explain, separately from the rate result, which it can. Construct validity is bounded where a measure stands in for something it cannot observe directly: self-reported success measures felt rather than actual completion, and the interface record measures which panels were configured rather than where a student's attention was. External validity is bounded by a single institution, two programmes, and small samples in both studies. None of these is remedied by the design chosen here, and each is stated where the affected claim is made rather than only in this section.
