\label{chap:methodology}
This chapter sets out the methodological frame within which the research questions posed in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} are addressed: the research paradigm that structures the work, the end-to-end process connecting its stages, and the scope, limitations, assumptions, and data-handling practices that bound how the findings should be read.

\section{Research Paradigm}
\label{sec:paradigm}
This thesis follows Munzner's nested model for visualisation design and validation~\cite{munzner2009}. The nested model organises a visualisation design process into four stratified levels: (1)~domain problem characterisation, (2)~data and task abstraction, (3)~visual encoding and interaction design, and (4)~algorithm design. A defining property of the model, central to how this thesis is structured, is that each level's output is the next level's input. Consequently, an error or unwarranted assumption at an upstream level cascades to every level downstream of it; the model therefore calls for each level to be validated on its own terms, rather than treating the finished system as a single object of evaluation~\cite{munzner2009}.

This thesis applies the model as follows. Level~1 (domain problem characterisation) is addressed in two interconnected parts: through the review of Related Work (Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}) and through the formative study (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}). Together, they define the problem space: the literature provides the theoretical foundation, while the study establishes the empirical reality. Level~2 (data/task abstraction) is addressed by the codebook-based translation of the needfinding results into a features-and-requirements specification (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}). Level~3 (visual encoding and interaction design) and level~4 (algorithm design) are addressed by the Design and Implementation chapters, respectively. Notably, Munzner's own level~2 construct is narrower than what Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements} produces. The model traditionally calls for generic operations and data types (e.g.,\ filter, sort, compare, applied to a table or a graph), whereas this thesis produces a comprehensive 13-feature requirements specification. This specification does not substitute level~2; rather, the generic operations and data types expected by the model are embedded within this richer document. Munzner explicitly anticipates such an adaptation~\cite{munzner2009}; because the nested model acts as an overarching macro-structure rather than a rigid toolkit. It naturally accommodates the integration of domain-specific methods, like standard requirements engineering, where its own prescriptions are narrow.

The two empirical studies in this thesis address distinct, non-overlapping spans of this model. The formative study focuses strictly on the pre-design phase. Operating within the problem space defined in level~1, it produces and establishes the specific requirements for the graph-table hybrid concept. Crucially, it does not evaluate the hybrid concept itself. Whether a combined graph-and-table interface is the optimal approach remains an upstream assumption (stated explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:overall-assumptions}), rather than a hypothesis tested empirically. In contrast, the evaluation study (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}) validates the encoding/interaction and algorithm levels, levels~3 and~4, of the resulting artefact. Crucially, because the nested model posits that downstream validation can expose upstream flaws, the findings from this evaluation inevitably reflect back on that foundational assumption, providing empirical feedback on whether the hybrid graph-table concept was the right approach. This fits the model's call to distinguish contributions by level and state upstream assumptions explicitly~\cite{munzner2009}. \section{Process Overview}
\label{sec:process-overview}
The end-to-end research process operationalises the nested model across six stages: (1)~a review of related work (Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}); (2)~the formative study, conducted with a non-functional prototype (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}); (3)~translation of findings into a requirements specification (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}); (4)~system design (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}); (5)~software implementation and testing (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}); and (6)~the evaluation study (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). Because each stage's specific methodology is documented in full within its respective chapter, this section outlines only the umbrella logistics of the two empirical anchor points.

Both studies were run with students enrolled in the two programmes in scope, both were moderated, both used a concurrent think-aloud protocol throughout the working phases, and both were recorded, transcribed, and analysed with codebook thematic analysis, each chapter documenting its own application (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}, Section~\ref{sec:analysis-method}; Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-analysis}).

They differ in purpose, and therefore in design. The \gterm{formative-study}{formative study} ($N=6$) was an elicitation interview series centred on an interactive but non-functional prototype; its output is what students need. The \gterm{evaluation-study}{evaluation study} (twelve sessions, eleven of them analysed) was a moderated observational study of the fully implemented system; its output is what students did. Each participant in it planned twice under a persona scenario, once with the curriculum graph unavailable and once with it, always in that order, so that the two runs are comparable across the cohort. The evaluation also adds a second, independent record for which the formative study has no counterpart, a frame-sampled log of the interface state, and it is that record which allows a reported outcome to be checked against an observed one. Each session followed one fixed sequence, documented together with the instruments and the coding procedure in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.



\section{Scope}
\label{sec:overall-scope}

Two kinds of boundary are set here. The first bounds the artefact, that is what the system deliberately does not do; the second bounds the evidence, that is the population and the curricula the findings cover.

\subsection*{Scope of the artefact}

Five boundaries are stated as numbered out-of-scope items, OOS1 to OOS5, and are referred to by those numbers throughout the thesis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OOS1, no automated planning.} The system guides students in planning; it does not plan on their behalf. This reflects both the intended role of the tool, decision support rather than decision automation, and the limited context available to the system to plan responsibly on a student's behalf.
    \item \textbf{OOS2, programme-level granularity.} Study planning is defined at the programme level throughout this thesis. Course-level planning, scheduling within a single course, deadline management, and appointment tracking are out of scope.
    \item \textbf{OOS3, no live connection to the institutional course or learning management system.} Stated again, with the excluded findings that justify it, in Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements} (Section~\ref{sec:overall-scope-refined}).
    \item \textbf{OOS4, no community features} such as ratings, discussion, or shared uploads. Justified in the same place as OOS3.
    \item \textbf{OOS5, recommendation quality is not a contribution.} The recommendation component exists to exercise the recommendation workflow, not to advance recommender research. It combines standard techniques over synthetically generated prior-student histories, and two of its six families could not fire at all during the evaluation study, so no statement about ranking quality follows from this work (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-recommender}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Scope of the evidence}

Two degree programmes at the Faculty of Informatics of TU~Wien are in scope: the Bachelor programme in Computer Science and the Master programme in Software Engineering. Every curriculum rule the system checks, every catalogue entry it serves, and every plan evaluated in either study belongs to one of these two programmes, and the compliance engine holds a separate rule set for each (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}). The two were chosen because they sit at different stages of a student's path, so that the requirements gathered span both a largely prescribed early curriculum and a largely elective later one.

\section{Limitations and Constraints}
\label{sec:overall-scope-detail}

Beyond the scope boundaries defined in Section~\ref{sec:overall-scope}, four specific methodological limitations bound how the empirical findings in this thesis should be applied:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Findings bounded to one faculty and two programmes.} The choice of scope is stated in Section~\ref{sec:overall-scope}; its consequence is that students with other disciplinary backgrounds, or at other institutions, may have planning needs this work did not encounter. All six formative-study participants, and the curriculum data the system was built on, come from TU~Wien's Faculty of Informatics.
    \item \textbf{Small sample sizes in both studies.} The formative study involved six participants; the evaluation study analyses eleven participants, which is smaller than would support strong statistical generalisation. Both studies are designed for depth of insight within a bounded population, not for population-level generalisability.
    \\item \\textbf{No directly comparable prior system.} To the best of our knowledge, no existing system combines a browsable curriculum graph with a schedule-managing table over one synchronised plan (Chapter~\\ref{chap:related-work}). The evidence motivating the hybrid concept is therefore inferential, drawn from adjacent systems and from general visualisation and HCI literature, rather than from research that tests hybrid interfaces of this kind against alternatives.
    \\item \\textbf{Single-researcher qualitative analysis.} Coding, theme development, adjudication between records, and severity rating were carried out by one researcher in both studies, without a second coder or a formal inter-coder reliability check. The two studies mitigate this differently: the formative study's codebook thematic analysis does not require multiple coders (Chapter~\\ref{chap:formative-study}, Section~\\ref{sec:analysis-method}), and the evaluation study checks a reported outcome against an independently produced observational record rather than against a second reading of the same one. Neither substitutes for an independent coder.
\end{itemize}

\section{Assumptions}
\label{sec:overall-assumptions}

Assumptions are premises this thesis takes as given before any study was run. They differ from the limitations above in what is at risk: a limitation bounds how far a finding travels, whereas an assumption, if false, makes a finding wrong. Munzner's nested model asks explicitly that assumptions held at levels above the one a work focuses on be stated rather than left implicit~\cite{munzner2009}. Two are load-bearing here.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The hybrid concept.} That a combined graph-and-table interface is worth building is motivated by literature (Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}), not tested here against a non-hybrid alternative. The formative study derives requirements \emph{within} the concept; it does not evaluate the concept itself.
    \item \textbf{What a curriculum encodes.} The graph-based literature reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} treats prerequisite sequencing as the planning-relevant structure of a curriculum, and this thesis carries that premise into its design rather than deriving it from the two curricula in scope. Those curricula publish little sequencing, so this is a premise the evaluation ends up testing rather than one it rests on undisturbed (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}; Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}, Section~\ref{sec:disc-related}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Expected Results}
\label{sec:expected-results}

This thesis produces three core outputs: a ranked features-and-requirements specification grounded in interview evidence (Chapter~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}); a designed and implemented graph-table hybrid realising it, with every design decision traceable to a requirement, a prototype assumption, common design knowledge, or literature (Chapters~\ref{chap:design}--\ref{chap:implementation}); and empirical findings on how that tool performs against the research questions (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). 
The third output has three strands, and they come from different records. The process account is derived from the frame-sampled interface log. The catalogue of usability problems and positive findings is coded from both records, the transcript and the interface log, each code carrying a participant-level frequency and mapped to an ISO~9241-11 dimension~\cite{iso9241_11}. The questionnaire results are a separate descriptive layer: they indicate where the weaknesses lie rather than carrying a finding themselves. Because the evaluation relies on a fixed scenario order and eleven analysed participants, all three are descriptive rather than causal. They describe how students approached planning; they cannot establish that a specific interface component caused a specific behaviour. Given these constraints, Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} (Section~\ref{sec:disc-generalisability}) examines which of these findings apply exclusively to the graph-table hybrid concept, and which might generalise to other systems.

\section{Data Handling}
\label{sec:ethics-overview}

Both studies followed the same consent, recording, and anonymisation protocol, compliant with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Participants provided explicit consent for data collection and processing (Art.~6(1)(a)) prior to their sessions, acknowledging their right to withdraw. Sessions were recorded and transcribed. Every analysis artefact, the transcripts, the frame-sampled state logs, the code matrix and the completion audit, refers to participants by code alone (I1--I6 for the formative study, P01--P12 for the evaluation study). The recordings themselves are working material rather than anonymised data: those captured as a full meeting window retain the participant's webcam tile. No frame of any recording is reproduced in this thesis, and all raw material, including the signed consent forms, is held on access-restricted storage and deleted at the conclusion of the thesis process.

To administer the evaluation study, a lightweight, purpose-built web application was developed separately from the main study-planning artefact (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). This tool presented the scenario briefs, administered post-task questionnaires, and logged responses. Developing a custom application was beneficial for data integrity: it ensured standardised session pacing, enabled precise, unified logging of participant inputs, and eliminated reliance on third-party survey platforms, thereby further securing participant data.

\section{Quality Criteria and Validity Strategy}
\label{sec:validity-overview}

Methodological rigour is systematically addressed across all research stages. Both studies analyse their qualitative material with codebook thematic analysis; the formative study sets out that choice in full and justifies it against the alternative traditions (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}, Section~\ref{sec:analysis-method}), and the evaluation study applies the same approach to its own material (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-analysis}). Design and implementation validity is secured through strict traceability: every design decision must trace back to a requirement, prior evidence, or an explicitly stated assumption (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}).

What is particular to the evaluation study is its second record. The frame-sampled interface log has no established methodological literature to draw on, and is not presented as though it had: the labelling scheme is fixed, reproduced in full in Section~\ref{app:eval-stagelog}, and was applied without reference to the transcripts, so that the two records can be compared rather than merged. Where they conflict, as when participants reported success on a plan that did not meet its brief, the observed record carries the claim.

Two threats bound what the evaluation study may claim, and each is restated where the affected claim is made:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Internal validity.} The fixed scenario order makes graph availability inseparable from second exposure and from the change of persona topic. Results that the order can explain are therefore reported separately from those it cannot, and only the latter are claimed as effects.
    \item \textbf{Construct validity.} Two measures stand in for something they cannot observe directly: self-reported success measures perceived rather than actual completion, and the interface log records which panels were configured rather than where a participant's attention was.
\end{itemize}
