origin~\cite[p.~94]{gotel_analysis_1994}.

Each theme was first read, together with the initial excerpts constituting it, to formulate a design implication, the guiding question being what the system must support if the need is to be addressed. Each theme, rather than a single utterance, is the operative expression of a stakeholder need: elicited material has to be interpreted, analysed and modelled before it can stand as a requirement~\cite[p.~39]{nuseibeh_requirements_2000}, and the theme is the unit at which that interpretation was recorded, with its excerpts behind it. One or more candidate requirements were then formulated per theme, written as system-oriented statements of capabilities or conditions and kept as implementation-independent as was reasonable: they specify \emph{what} the system should support, not \emph{how}. Closely related requirements were then grouped into \emph{\gterm{feature}{features}}, a feature being a coherent product capability bundling related requirements under one functional topic. %% REV (from here onwards it gets a little bit unclear again, try to make it clearer again (not longer!))
Where several themes converged on one capability their requirements were grouped into a shared feature; where one theme implied substantively different capabilities its requirements contributed to more than one. A requirement count is therefore not tied one-to-one to its parent theme. Where a feature carries an intentional exclusion, a short delineation records it, consistent with requirements-engineering practice, in which not all elicited needs are taken forward unchanged but must be bounded in a form manageable for development. %% REV (is this true?, i think of these four also some features are partly implemented, so not all req of them but a view. \section{Scope Refinement for Requirements Derivation}
\label{sec:overall-scope-refined}
The five scope boundaries that bound the artefact, OOS1 to OOS5, are defined in the Methodology chapter (Section~\ref{sec:overall-scope}). Two of them rest on evidence that only this chapter's excluded findings supply, and are therefore restated here with that justification:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OOS3:} No live connection to the institutional course management or learning management system (CMS/LMS).
    \item \textbf{OOS4:} No community features (e.g., ratings, discussion, chat, or upload of shared resources).
\end{itemize}

All five boundaries apply throughout Section~\ref{sec:resulting-features} and are referenced there and in later chapters by these same numbers.

The metric is a sum rather than a mean, and that choice is deliberate. Summing prevalences means a feature carrying seven themes tends to outrank one carrying two even where the individual themes are less intensely held, so the score rewards breadth of evidence alongside depth: a feature that many separate concerns converge on is treated as more urgent than one that fewer participants raised more insistently. Normalising by theme count would order the specification differently, and the reader should be aware of it, but the ranking is not load-bearing for any claim in this thesis. It was used once, to order implementation work, and no finding in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} or Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} depends on where a feature sits in it.

The ranking separates the specification into three bands. The four highest-scoring features (scores 15--20) are the planning substrate: getting curriculum data into the system without manual transcription (FEATURE-001), showing progress against the programme at three granularities (FEATURE-002), enforcing the curriculum's own rules (FEATURE-003), and moving a course from exploration in the graph to commitment in the table (FEATURE-004). These four define the hybrid graph--table concept that the rest of the thesis builds and tests. A middle band (scores 9--14) adds the judgement layer participants described around that substrate: semester feasibility (FEATURE-005), explainable recommendation (FEATURE-007), stage-appropriate onboarding (FEATURE-006), and replanning under disruption (FEATURE-008). The remaining five (scores 3--6) concern how the representation behaves once it is populated: density control (FEATURE-009), graph interpretability (FEATURE-010), visual and affective load (FEATURE-011), non-destructive recommendation display (FEATURE-012), and user-defined spatial grouping (FEATURE-013).

Two features contain requirements that were derived from the data but not carried into the implemented system, because of resource and time constraints during development: FEATURE-005, Req.~3 (self-assessed readiness profiles) and FEATURE-013, Reqs.~1, 3, and~4 (named, cross-view groups). Both are stated in the respective Delineation in Section~\ref{app:feature-specs} and are taken up as shortfalls in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}. Every other exclusion recorded in a Delineation follows from a scope boundary, %% REV (outdated oos1 is missing right?) OOS2 to OOS4, or from a design decision stated there, not from what was built.
