\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis set out to design, build and evaluate a hybrid graph-table interface for programme-level study planning, and to learn from students how such an interface is used. It did so in three steps that answer its three research questions: a formative study that produced a specification of what students need (Chapter~\ref{chap:formative-study}), a design and an implementation that realise it (Chapters~\ref{chap:design} and~\ref{chap:implementation}), and an evaluation that observed eleven students planning with the result, once without the curriculum graph and once with it (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}), interpreted in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}. This chapter states the answers, restates what the thesis contributes, and names the work that follows.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:concl-rq-answers}

This section states what each research question was answered with, and how completely; the interpretation those answers rest on is Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.

\paragraph{Research Question 1: students' needs and the resulting requirements for a hybrid graph-table interface supporting programme-level planning and the understanding of curriculum structure and course dependencies.}
The answer is the specification of Section~\ref{chap:from-themes-to-requirements}: 13 features carrying 55 numbered requirements, each traceable through a theme to the interview excerpts that motivated it, and ranked by an importance score that governed what was built first. It is answered on the evidence of six students at one faculty, which bounds it in the way Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} states: the specification documents what this population needed, not what students in general need. Two parts of the question are answered less completely than the rest. The needs concerning course dependencies were elicited and specified (Feature~\ref{feat:003}, Req.~2 and~3), but the curricula in scope turned out to encode little sequence to act on (Chapter~\ref{chap:design}, Section~\ref{sec:graph-ltr}): the Bachelor curriculum the study planned in publishes two orderings between named courses and enforces neither, so the evaluated system's overlay, which draws hard prerequisites only, rendered nothing at all and the two reached students as advisory warnings in the compliance layer; the requirement is met on a relation that is nearly empty. And the specification is a statement of needs rather than a validated instrument: it was not tested against an alternative elicitation, and four of its requirements were not carried into the implemented system, which the delineations of Features~\ref{feat:005} and~\ref{feat:013} record.

\paragraph{Research Question 2: how a hybrid interface combining graph-based visualisation with table-based planning can be designed and implemented from those requirements.}
The answer is the artefact together with the reasoning recorded alongside it. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} answers the design half by deriving each decision from a requirement, a prototype assumption, published guidance, or a stated design judgement, and by naming the alternative it was weighed against where one was live; Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} answers the implementation half with a working system whose central structural decision, a single authoritative plan model from which both views and all feedback are derived, is what makes the two views projections of one plan rather than two plans to reconcile. The question asks \emph{how} such an interface can be built, and a single realisation cannot establish that this is the only way or the best way to build one; what it describes is one way it can be built, and at what cost in specific decisions. Two of those costs are visible in the evaluation: the centre panel carries one view at a time, so work that spans both runs through the Parking Stage; and the overlay rule that draws only enforced orderings excluded the Bachelor's two advisory ones, leaving participants an empty overlay.

\paragraph{Research Question 3: how students plan with the hybrid interface, and what the graph-based view contributes to their planning.}
How students plan: under this tool, planning is a single checklist-driven loop, the same for every participant, in which the Dashboard's missing-requirements list is read, a candidate is found and held, placed, and the list re-read; the loop's period does not change with the graph, under the fixed order that precludes attributing the constancy to it (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-order}). What the graph contributes: it substitutes for the catalogue at the query step of that loop rather than adding a station, and it does not support the commit step, for the structural reason that it shows no semester dimension; its value sat in the query step and largely in its filters. Read through the three lenses Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} names, the answer holds as follows. The process was read from the path through the interface rather than from elapsed time, because at eleven participants and with a fixed order no timing could have been attributed to the tool. The plans produced were read from an audit and not from what students reported, and the two disagree: students met the constraints the tool can represent in seventeen of the twenty-one resolved runs (one of the twenty-two could not be resolved, two of its semester lanes being unreadable in the recording) and the full brief in thirteen of them, while seven of the twenty-one runs their authors rated a success did not meet the brief, four of those seven because no reduced-load semester was planned at all. Satisfaction was read from self-report and is high and consistent, with pragmatic quality above hedonic and perspicuity the weakest scale, which is consistent with the terminology and learnability problems the observational record describes; with the author as moderator and no comparison condition it carries an upward bias, and its informative part is the profile rather than the level. One component of the interface is answered more narrowly than the rest. The Recommendation Panel was measured and its use characterised, but every family answers from what a student supplies, from static catalogue and curriculum data, or from a synthetic cohort, and the deliberately simple matching behind them was never the object of study (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-recommender}). So what was evaluated is the recommendation \emph{workflow}, not the value of the suggestions it carried.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:concl-contributions}

The contributions stated in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}) stand as claimed, with the scope the answers above give them. The requirements specification is a statement of what six students at one faculty needed, traceable to their words. The tool is a working hybrid planner whose central decisions are recorded with their costs, and it is a platform for further evaluation as much as a result. The process account describes, at the level of every sampled frame, how the eleven participants planned with a graph-and-table interface, where the graph enters that process, and how far the plans they produced matched what they and the tool reported about them: seven of the eight runs that missed their brief were rated a success by the participant who produced them while the tool reported no missing requirements in all twenty-two, a finding about this tool's completion signal whose mechanism may be worth testing in other checklist-driven tools.
One property follows from the three taken together. Because the specification was elicited without a fixed hypothesis about what the tool should contain, and the finished tool was then observed at the level of the interface, a component can be read in both directions: from the student statement that motivated it, through the feature and requirement it became, to the share of the planning process it took. The Dashboard runs the whole length, from an interview asking \enquote{where am I right now?} (T16) to the checklist every session was driven from (E-G15, 11/11). The chain is traceable rather than causal, and its last link rests on eleven participants under a fixed condition order.

Each is bounded by the limitations Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} states, and each is offered as the exploratory start of a line of work rather than its conclusion.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl-future}

The implemented system is a platform on which a programme of evaluation can be built, and the study reported here is its first direction, not its only one. The same tool supports evaluation along axes this thesis did not pursue: the quality and acceptance of the recommendation channels considered on their own, the effect of the compliance layer on plan correctness under controlled prior knowledge, longitudinal use across a real enrolment period rather than a laboratory session, comparison against the institutional tools students currently use, and larger and broader cohorts than eleven computing students. Within the direction this thesis did take, five questions follow directly from the findings.

First, the study yields a falsifiable design hypothesis it could not itself test. If the value participants attributed to the graph-on condition is carried by the filters rather than by the node--link view, then bringing the full filter set into the table should render a separate graph mode largely optional. The substitution result sharpens the prediction: with filters in the table, the query station should stay in the table rather than migrating to the graph, and the graph's occupancy should fall towards the catalogue's level in the graph-on condition. This is also the test the hybrid concept itself still owes.

Second, the conditions this study ran admit two variations it could not. Separating practice from graph availability needs a between-subjects arm that repeats the task without the graph, A$\to$A$'$, run alongside the A$\to$B arm reported here: the combined between- and within-subjects design Greenwald recommends where a treatment may interact with practice~\cite[p.~316]{greenwald1976within}. The other variation is a graph-first condition, which is a different experiment rather than this one reversed. With the graph present during initial learning, it may be folded into the base loop rather than remaining an excursion, or, on the evidence of the most junior participant, it may overload a student who has no simpler fallback workflow. Both are testable in a powered study and neither is answerable at this sample size.

Third, the problem register of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} (Table~\ref{tab:problems}) is a development agenda. The problems most visible in the observed process are the reduced-load semester, the enforced workload band, the per-lane term indicator, and per-lane load inside the graph. Of these, the term indicator is the one change predicted to move the placement-rework measure that did not differ between conditions, and lane totals in the graph are the one change that would let commit support be tested rather than found absent; implementing them makes the completion signal a test object in its own right.

Fourth, the process account should be repeated as a log study. The plan model already stamps every change (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, Section~\ref{sec:impl-frontend}), so an event log written by the tool would give exact entries, dwell, placements and rejections for every session with no sampling and no labelling, and it would scale to the cohort this study could not recruit: a few hundred students planning their own programmes over a semester rather than eleven planning a persona's in an hour. That is the study that could turn the loop, the substitution and the calibration gap from descriptions into estimates, and it would answer with data what this thesis can only pose as its first hypothesis, whether the graph earns its place once the table has its filters.

Fifth, prerequisite visibility is worth posing as a question about what is worth drawing rather than as a design to build. Four participants asked for ordering information at the point of placement (E-P44, 4/11), but each curriculum states only two orderings between named courses, enforced in the Master and advisory in the Bachelor. The question is therefore not whether students can read prerequisite edges: it is whether four such edges across two programmes change how a plan is built, and whether a view of the eligibility gates these curricula do encode, StEOP and core-before-elective, would serve the same need better than a view of sequence.

Beyond these, the patterns offered here as exploratory, in particular the expertise-moderated use of recommendations, warrant a powered study with an explicit measure of the relevant individual difference and a sample drawn beyond computing programmes.

This thesis set out to learn how students plan with a hybrid graph-table interface and what the graph-based view contributes to that planning. What it found is a single checklist-driven loop, the same for every participant, in which the graph takes over the query step rather than adding one, and a completion signal students read as a verdict on their task when it speaks only for the curriculum's own rules.
